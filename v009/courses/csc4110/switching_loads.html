A pin cannot power anything bigger than an LED, but it can control a switch that can. This lesson covers the three switches in your kit: a transistor for fast or proportional loads, a relay for rare or isolated ones, and an H-bridge for motors that must reverse. Along the way you meet the two things that destroy beginners’ circuits: too much current through a pin, and the voltage spike from a coil.

SAM lens. The pin only signals; transistors, relays and H-bridges are the Actuator's muscles. The safety habits here (flyback diodes, separate supplies, try/finally) protect the agent from its own actions.

Why a Pin Cannot Drive a Motor

A GPIO pin is a signal, not a power source. The Pico’s pins are meant for a few milliamps (4 mA is the default drive strength and 12 mA the most you can configure), and the whole chip has a total limit as well. An LED at 6 mA is fine. A fan, a pump, a relay coil or a motor wants ten to a hundred times more. The solution is always the same idea: let the pin control a switch, and let the switch carry the load’s current from a supply that can provide it.

Typical currents for the loads in your kit. These are round numbers to guide the choice; the real figure for your part is on its label, or you can measure it with a multimeter in series. A motor draws several times more when it is stalled than when it is turning.

Part 1. The Transistor as a Switch

A bipolar transistor (BJT) has three leads: base, collector and emitter. A small current into the base lets a much larger current flow from the collector to the emitter. Used as a switch, you push enough base current to turn it fully on (it is then saturated, with only a few tenths of a volt across it) or none at all (off). Your kit has an NPN type, the S8050, and a PNP type, the S8550. We use the NPN as a low-side switch: the load connects to the supply, the transistor connects the load to ground.

A transistor switch for a load that needs about 100 mA. The Pico pin pushes a small base current through a resistor; the large load current flows from the battery, through the load and the transistor, to ground. The diode across the load (stripe toward +V) is explained in Part 2.

Choosing the Base Resistor

Datasheets list a current gain (hFE) of a few hundred for the S8050: a base current of 1 mA could control up to a few hundred mA of collector current. But that is the gain in the amplifying region. To use the transistor as a switch you ignore it and push far more base current than the gain alone would need, so the transistor is driven hard into saturation. A good rule is to design for a forced gain of 20 to 50: the collector current is only that many times the base current.

  1. Decide the load current. A small fan: Ic = 100 mA.
  2. Choose a base current that the pin can supply. With a forced gain of 38, Ib = 100 mA / 38 = 2.6 mA, well under the 4 mA drive limit.
  3. Compute the resistor. The base-emitter junction drops about 0.7 V, so Rb = (3.3 − 0.7) V / 2.6 mA = 1 kΩ, a value in your kit. (With 470 Ω you get 5.5 mA, more than the default drive strength; stay at 1 kΩ.)
  4. Check the transistor: with the supply at 5 V it dissipates about Vce(sat) × Ic = 0.3 V × 100 mA = 30 mW, which is nothing. The resistor wastes (3.3 − 0.7) V × 2.6 mA = 6.8 mW.
  5. Measure. Put the multimeter across collector and emitter with the load on: you should read well under 0.5 V. If you read more, the transistor is not saturating; lower the resistor value, or use a transistor with more gain.

The same calculation tells you where the method stops. A load of 500 mA with a forced gain of 38 needs 13 mA of base current, which the pin cannot give; that load needs a driver chip (the H-bridge below), or a second transistor, or a logic-level MOSFET, which we do not use in this course.

Part 2. Flyback: The Spike from a Coil

Motors, relay coils and solenoids are inductors: they store energy in a magnetic field, and an inductor resists a sudden change in its current. When the transistor turns off, the current in the coil must go somewhere. With nowhere to go, the coil’s voltage swings upward until something breaks down, often the transistor itself, and a spike of tens of volts (sometimes far more) appears at the collector. It is also noise that crosses the wiring into your Pico, causing resets and bad ADC readings.

The voltage at the transistor’s collector when the load is switched off (schematic, not to scale). Without a diode the coil makes a tall spike. With the diode the spike is clamped to one diode drop above the supply.

The cure costs ten cents: a flyback diode (also called a freewheeling or snubber diode) connected across the coil, backwards relative to the supply. It does nothing while the load is on. When the transistor switches off, the coil’s current finds a path around through the diode and fades away harmlessly. Orientation matters: the stripe (the cathode) goes to the +V side. If you put it in the wrong way round you have built a short circuit from the supply to ground through the diode the moment you turn the power on, so check before you connect the battery. Any small diode in your kit is fine for these currents (a 1N4007 or a 1N4148).

Motor driver modules and relay modules already include these diodes. A bare motor, fan or pump on your own transistor does not, and a PWM-driven load switches off thousands of times per second, so the diode is not optional.

Part 3. Relays

A relay is a switch operated by an electromagnet: a coil (the input side) moves a metal contact (the output side). The two sides are electrically separate, which is why relays are used for loads on a different voltage, or loads that must be isolated from the controller. Three terminals on the output: COM (common), NO (normally open: connected to COM only while the coil is on) and NC (normally closed: connected to COM while the coil is off).

Relay or transistor? The relay is for loads that change rarely; the transistor is for anything that changes quickly or in proportion.

The kit’s relay arrives on a module: a small board with a driver transistor, a flyback diode, an indicator LED and usually an optocoupler, so the Pico pin drives it directly. Two details trip people up. First, most modules are active low: the relay turns on when the pin is low. Second, a pin that is configured as an output defaults to low, so a careless program makes the relay click on for a moment at start-up. The Relay class below sets the pin’s off level in the same call that makes it an output.

relay.py MicroPython
from machine import Pin class Relay: """A relay module. Most are active low: the coil is on when the pin is low.""" def __init__(self, pin, active_low=True): self.al = active_low # Give the pin its OFF level at the moment it becomes an output, # so the relay does not click when the program starts. self.pin = Pin(pin, Pin.OUT, value=1 if active_low else 0) def on(self): self.pin.value(0 if self.al else 1) def off(self): self.pin.value(1 if self.al else 0) def is_on(self): return self.pin.value() == (0 if self.al else 1)

Only switch low voltage in this course. The module’s contacts may be printed with mains ratings, but the board is not an enclosure, the screw terminals are exposed, and wiring mains power is for a qualified electrician, not for a lab session. Use the contacts to switch your battery pack or a lamp on its own low-voltage supply, and nothing else.

Because a relay clicks and wears, protect it from a program that toggles it hundreds of times a second by mistake (a sensor hovering at a threshold will do exactly that). Exercise 4 adds a minimum dwell time.

Part 4. Switching with PWM

A transistor is fast enough to follow the PWM from the previous lesson, so the average power in the load follows the duty cycle: 50 % duty gives roughly half the speed of a fan, or half the brightness of a lamp. Two choices matter. Frequency: a low frequency makes motors and fans buzz or stutter, while a very high one makes the transistor spend more time in the in-between region where it is hot; 1 kHz is a sensible starting point and 20 kHz is silent. The motor’s response: a fan will not turn at all below a minimum duty (it needs a certain torque to start), so you will find a dead zone that your control code must jump over. Load wraps this, with the active-low option for relay-style boards.

load.py MicroPython
from machine import Pin, PWM class Load: """Something switched by a transistor (or a relay module): 0..100 percent.""" def __init__(self, pin, freq=1000, active_low=False): self.inv = active_low self.pwm = PWM(Pin(pin), freq=freq, duty_u16=0) self.off() def set(self, pct): pct = max(0, min(100, pct)) duty = int(pct * 65535 / 100) self.pwm.duty_u16(65535 - duty if self.inv else duty) def off(self): self.set(0)

Part 5. Driving a Motor in Both Directions

A transistor switch turns a motor on and off, one direction only. To run it forward and backward you need an H-bridge: four switches arranged in an H with the motor as the crossbar. Closing the diagonal pair in one pattern pushes current through the motor one way; closing the other diagonal reverses it. You have already seen why the two switches on the same side must never close together.

The three working states of an H-bridge. Diagonal switches give forward or reverse. Closing the two lower switches shorts the motor’s terminals, which resists spinning and so brakes it. With all four open (IN1=0, IN2=0) the motor coasts.

Your kit’s TA6586 module contains the four switches, the logic that prevents the forbidden state, and the diodes. You give it two logic inputs and a motor supply. The truth table below is the usual one for this family of chips; verify it on your own module with the bench test, because modules differ and a wiring mix-up looks the same as a different truth table.

The H-bridge truth table. The Motor class from earlier PWM work implements exactly this: one input carries the PWM and the other is held low.
motor.py MicroPython
from machine import Pin, PWM class Motor: """One motor on an H-bridge driver such as the TA6586: two inputs, IN1 and IN2.""" def __init__(self, in1, in2, freq=2000): self.in1 = PWM(Pin(in1), freq=freq, duty_u16=0) self.in2 = PWM(Pin(in2), freq=freq, duty_u16=0) def speed(self, pct): """-100 (full reverse) .. 0 (coast) .. 100 (full forward).""" pct = max(-100, min(100, pct)) duty = int(abs(pct) / 100 * 65535) if pct > 0: self.in1.duty_u16(duty); self.in2.duty_u16(0) elif pct < 0: self.in1.duty_u16(0); self.in2.duty_u16(duty) else: self.in1.duty_u16(0); self.in2.duty_u16(0) # coast def brake(self): self.in1.duty_u16(65535); self.in2.duty_u16(65535)

Wiring and Power

Wire list for one motor. The motor has its own battery; the Pico supplies the two control signals. Place a 100 µF capacitor across the battery pins near the driver if you have one, and a 0.1 µF ceramic across the motor terminals to calm its electrical noise.
  • Separate supply, common ground. The same rule as the servo: the load’s current never passes through the Pico, but their grounds are joined.
  • Stall current. Check the driver’s current rating against the motor’s stalled current, not only its running current, and run the motor briefly when you test.
  • Supply voltage. Read the driver module’s printed voltage range and stay inside it; check what voltage the motor is rated for as well. A 3 V motor on a 6 V supply gets hot.
  • Soft start. A motor draws a surge when it starts. Ramping the speed up in steps (see the bench test) is gentler on the battery and keeps the Pico from resetting.

Part 6. Bench Test

Wire the module and run this on a motor with nothing attached to its shaft. The try/finally is a habit worth forming now: whatever happens in the program, the last thing it does is turn the motor off. A robot whose program crashes while the motor is on keeps driving. In Unit 2 you will add the same fail-safe for a lost network connection.

bench.py MicroPython
from time import sleep_ms from motor import Motor def ramp(motor, target, start=0, step=5, ms=20): """Change speed in small steps instead of one jump: gentler on the supply.""" s = start while s != target: if abs(target - s) < step: s = target else: s += step if target > s else -step motor.speed(s) sleep_ms(ms) m = Motor(14, 15) try: ramp(m, 60) # soft start forward sleep_ms(1500) ramp(m, 0, start=60) # slow down sleep_ms(500) ramp(m, -60) # reverse, through zero sleep_ms(1500) m.brake() sleep_ms(500) finally: m.speed(0) # whatever happens, leave the motor off
  • Does forward spin the way you expect? If not, swap the two motor leads or swap IN1 and IN2 in the constructor; do not edit the logic.
  • Does the brake feel firmer than the coast? Turn the shaft by hand during each.
  • Find the lowest speed at which the motor starts. This dead zone is why controllers that drive a motor often need a minimum output.
  • Watch the Pico: if it resets when the motor starts, add the capacitor and a longer soft start before trying again.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Choose the Resistor

The base-emitter drop leaves 3.3 − 0.7 = 2.6 V across the resistor. For no more than 4 mA you need R ≥ 2.6 / 0.004 = 650 Ω. So 330 Ω is out: it would ask for 2.6 / 330 = 7.9 mA, too much for the pin. With 1 kΩ the base current is 2.6 mA, and the forced gain is 150 / 2.6 = 58.

Whether 1 kΩ saturates depends on the real current gain at 150 mA, which is lower than the headline figure on the datasheet. It is plausible but with little margin, so measure the voltage across the transistor: below about 0.5 V and it is saturated. Otherwise you need a transistor with more gain, or a driver chip.

Exercise 2: Read the Motor Class

speed(60): duty = int(0.60 × 65535) = 39321 on IN1 and 0 on IN2: forward at 60 %. speed(-40): IN1 gets 0, IN2 gets int(0.40 × 65535) = 26214: reverse at 40 %. brake(): both are 65535 (both inputs held high), the brake row of the truth table.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: Pick the Switch

(a) Pin directly, with a resistor: 6 mA is within the drive limit. (b) Transistor with PWM: it needs speeds, which a relay cannot give, and one direction only. (c) A relay works well here (rare switching, different supply, no speed control), and a transistor would also be correct; the relay isolates the supply. (d) H-bridge: only a bridge reverses the current. (e) Relay module: it is the one with electrical isolation between the Pico’s side and the lamp’s side.

Advanced

Exercise 4: Do Not Click Too Fast
safe_relay.py MicroPython
from time import ticks_ms, ticks_diff from relay import Relay class SafeRelay(Relay): """A relay that refuses to change state more often than every min_ms.""" def __init__(self, pin, active_low=True, min_ms=2000): super().__init__(pin, active_low) self.min_ms = min_ms self.last = ticks_ms() def set(self, want): """Returns True if the relay is now in the wanted state.""" if want == self.is_on(): return True if ticks_diff(ticks_ms(), self.last) < self.min_ms: return False # too soon: ask again later self.on() if want else self.off() self.last = ticks_ms() return True
test_safe.py MicroPython
# fake clock; the relay was created at t = 10 000 ms # t=12100 set(True) -> True (2.1 s since creation) relay on # t=13000 set(False) -> False (only 0.9 s since the change) relay stays on # t=14200 set(False) -> True (2.1 s since the change) relay off

The first request is allowed because more than min_ms has passed since creation. The off request 0.9 s later is refused, and the relay stays on; asked again after 2.1 s it succeeds. A request that asks for the state the relay is already in always succeeds immediately. The caller keeps asking every pass of its loop, so the relay follows the sensor, but never faster than once per two seconds. Using ticks_diff keeps it correct when the millisecond counter wraps.

Paper Practice: Answers

Recall

P1. Pin Limits

A few milliamps (4 mA default drive, 12 mA at most). A motor wants tens to hundreds of times more; the pin must control a switch that carries the load current from a separate supply.

P2. Saturation

Driven fully on, with enough base current that the collector current is set by the load, not the transistor; only a few tenths of a volt (about 0.2 to 0.3 V) across collector and emitter.

P3. Relay Terminals

COM is the common contact. NO (normally open) connects to COM only while the coil is energised. NC (normally closed) connects to COM while the coil is off.

P4. H-Bridge Table

Forward, reverse, brake (terminals shorted), coast (all switches open).

Apply

P5. Base Resistor

Ib = 150 / 40 = 3.75 mA; Rb = (3.3 − 0.7) / 3.75 mA = 693 Ω. Choose 680 Ω: it gives 3.8 mA, still under the 4 mA default drive. 1 kΩ gives only 2.6 mA, a forced gain of 58, which risks not saturating.

P6. Dissipation

0.3 × 0.150 = 45 mW. The resistor: 2.6 V × 3.8 mA ≈ 10 mW.

P7. Too Big

500 / 38 = 13 mA, more than a pin can give. Use a driver chip (the H-bridge module), a second transistor stage, or a logic-level MOSFET.

P8. Dead Zone

duty = 0 if c == 0 else 30 + 0.7 × c (so 1 % maps to about 30.7 % and 100 % to 100 %).

Analyze and Design

P9. Flyback

The coil resists the sudden change in current; with nowhere to go, its voltage spikes to tens of volts or more, which can destroy the transistor and inject noise that resets the Pico. A diode across the coil, stripe (cathode) toward +V, gives the current a path to circulate and decay; the spike is clamped to one diode drop above the supply.

P10. Start-up Click

Configuring the pin as an output defaults it to low, which turns an active-low relay on. Set the off level (high) in the same call that makes the pin an output, as the Relay class does.

P11. Resets on Start

Motor powered from the same supply as the Pico (separate the supplies, join the grounds); the start surge dips the supply (soft start: ramp the speed; add a 100 µF capacitor near the driver); electrical noise from the motor (0.1 µF across the motor terminals, short wires, flyback diodes).

P12. Pump Switch

Low-side NPN: 12 V → pump → collector; emitter → ground; Pico pin → Rb → base; 12 V supply ground joined to Pico GND. Ib = 80 / 30 = 2.7 mA; Rb = 2.6 V / 2.7 mA ≈ 975 Ω, so 1 kΩ. Safety: a flyback diode across the pump (stripe to +12 V); a common ground; try/finally that turns the pump off.
