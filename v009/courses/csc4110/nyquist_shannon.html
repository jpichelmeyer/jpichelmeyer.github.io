The sampling lesson gave the rule of thumb: sample faster than twice the signal's frequency. This lesson makes that precise, shows exactly what goes wrong when you break it, and shows the one place where the fix has to happen (before the ADC, not after).

Part 1. The Theorem, Stated Carefully

If a signal contains no frequencies above fmax, then sampling it at a rate fs greater than 2 × fmax captures it completely: the original wave could in principle be rebuilt exactly from the samples. Half the sampling rate, fs / 2, is called the Nyquist frequency.

Two phrases matter. "No frequencies above fmax" is a statement about the whole signal, including noise and interference you did not intend to measure. And "greater than" is strict: a wave sampled exactly twice per cycle can be missed entirely if the samples happen to land on its zero crossings.

Part 2. Aliasing: Where the Missing Frequencies Go

A frequency above the Nyquist limit does not simply disappear. It folds back and appears at a lower frequency, mixed in with the real signal:

  • apparent frequency = |f − k × fs|, using the whole number k that makes the result smallest.
Apparent frequency against true frequency when sampling at 100 Hz. Up to 50 Hz the two agree; beyond it the line folds back, so every faster signal disguises itself as a slower one.

Here a 9 Hz signal measured at 10 Hz is read as |9 − 10| = 1 Hz. Every dot is an accurate measurement; the lie is in the pattern.

A 9 Hz wave measured only 10 times a second. Each dot is a perfectly accurate reading, yet joined up they trace a slow 1 Hz wave that does not exist.

Part 3. The Fix Must Happen Before the ADC

Once a signal has been sampled, an alias is indistinguishable from a genuine slow wave. No software filter, however clever, can tell them apart afterwards. The defence is an anti-aliasing filter: an analog low-pass filter that weakens everything above the Nyquist frequency before the ADC sees it. The simplest one is a resistor and a capacitor:

A resistor and a capacitor in front of the ADC pin form a low-pass filter. It is the standard defence against aliasing, because it acts on the analog signal before it is sampled.

For most slow classroom sensors (temperature, light, a dial) nothing fast is present to alias, so you can skip the filter. It becomes essential for anything that could carry fast interference: a long wire acting as an antenna, a motor nearby, a sound or vibration sensor.

Part 4. Practical Choices

  • Oversample. Choose a rate several times the minimum, 2.5 to 10 times the highest frequency you care about, because real filters are not perfectly sharp.
  • Average after oversampling. Sampling faster than needed and then averaging blocks of samples (a moving average) both reduces noise and tames anything near the Nyquist limit.
  • Sample on a clock, not by guessing. The loop below keeps the average rate correct even though each pass takes a slightly different time:
main.py MicroPython
from machine import ADC, Pin from time import ticks_us, ticks_add, ticks_diff adc = ADC(Pin(26)) PERIOD_US = 5000 # 200 samples per second next_t = ticks_us() while True: sample = adc.read_u16() # ... use the sample ... next_t = ticks_add(next_t, PERIOD_US) # schedule from the plan, while ticks_diff(next_t, ticks_us()) > 0: # not from "now", so pass # timing errors never pile up

Scheduling each sample from the planned time, not from when the previous one actually finished, stops tiny delays accumulating into a slowly wrong sampling rate.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Where Does It Fold?

Apparent = |f − k·fs|: 70 → |70 − 100| = 30 Hz; 130 → |130 − 100| = 30 Hz; 160 → |160 − 200| = 40 Hz; 100 → 0 Hz, a signal exactly at the sampling rate looks perfectly constant.

Exercise 2: Choose a Sampling Rate

The theoretical minimum is just over 80 Hz. In practice choose 2.5 to 10 times the highest frequency, so 100 to 400 Hz, which leaves margin for real-world filters that are not perfectly sharp.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: An Alias Calculator
alias.py MicroPython
def alias(f, fs): k = round(f / fs) # nearest multiple of the sampling rate return abs(f - k * fs) for f in (70, 130, 160, 100): print(f, "->", alias(f, 100))

Output: 30, 30, 40, 0, matching the hand calculation.

Advanced

Exercise 4: Cancelling Mains Hum Exactly

Four samples at 200 Hz span 20 ms, exactly one full cycle of 50 Hz. Averaging over a whole number of cycles makes the positive and negative halves cancel, so the 50 Hz component averages to zero. (A moving average of length N at sample rate fs has a null at fs / N; here 200 / 4 = 50 Hz.) Choosing N so a null lands on the interference is a standard trick.
