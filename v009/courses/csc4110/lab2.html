This lab measures real sensors instead of trusting them. You will find out how noisy your light and temperature sensors are, test the 1/√N rule against your own data, put a number on the lag that filtering costs, and then deliberately break the sampling rule to see aliasing happen on your own board.

SAM lens. this lab measures your Perceptor. Noise, filtering and aliasing are all properties of the path from the world to a percept, and the numbers you record here decide how much the Decider can trust it.

Helper Code

lab2_tools.py MicroPython
from machine import ADC from time import ticks_us, ticks_add, ticks_diff, sleep_us from math import sqrt def collect(read, hz, n): """n readings at a steady rate. Returns (values, overruns).""" period = 1_000_000 // hz deadline = ticks_us() values, overruns = [], 0 for _ in range(n): values.append(read()) deadline = ticks_add(deadline, period) wait = ticks_diff(deadline, ticks_us()) if wait > 0: sleep_us(wait) else: overruns += 1 return values, overruns def mean_std(values): m = sum(values) / len(values) var = sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in values) / len(values) return m, sqrt(var) def apparent_hz(values, hz): """Rough frequency of a repeating signal: count upward crossings of the middle.""" lo, hi = min(values), max(values) mid, band = (lo + hi) / 2, (hi - lo) / 6 # hysteresis so noise is not counted count, high = 0, values[0] > mid for v in values: if not high and v > mid + band: high = True count += 1 elif high and v < mid - band: high = False return count * hz / len(values)

collect is the steady-rate sampler from the sampling lesson. mean_std gives the average and the standard deviation, the usual measure of noise size. apparent_hz is a crude way to see how fast a signal seems to repeat; the aliasing experiment uses it.

Part A. How Noisy Is Each Sensor?

  1. Leave the LED off and the room lighting steady. Collect 200 readings at 100 Hz from the LDR and print mean_std. Record the mean and the standard deviation in counts (shift the 16-bit values right by 4 first so that you are working in true ADC counts).
  2. Turn the LED on fully (set its pin high) and repeat. Is the noise the same in the dark and in the light?
  3. Repeat for the thermistor. Convert the mean to degrees with ntc_celsius and compare with a room thermometer or your phone.

Part B. Filter It

  1. Pass the same 200 LDR readings through MovingAverage(8), MovingAverage(32) and EMA(0.2) (offline, from the list you collected) and report the standard deviation of each output over the last 100 values.
  2. Compare the measured noise reduction with the prediction (1 / √N). Do they agree?
  3. Measure the cost. Take the filter into live use: run EMA(0.2) on the LDR and cover the sensor suddenly. Count how many samples the output takes to reach 90 % of its final change. Repeat for MovingAverage(32).

Part C. Break the Sampling Rate

The aliasing experiment. The LED blinks at 9 Hz, faster than your eye can count. Sampling at 100 Hz sees the true blink; sampling at 10 Hz sees an invented 1 Hz one.
alias_demo.py MicroPython
from machine import Pin, PWM, ADC led = PWM(Pin(13), freq=9, duty_u16=32768) # 9 Hz, half on (the lowest PWM rates are about 8 Hz) ldr = ADC(26) for hz in (100, 10): vals, over = collect(lambda: ldr.read_u16() >> 4, hz, hz * 3) # 3 seconds print(hz, "Hz sampling ->", round(apparent_hz(vals, hz), 1), "Hz apparent, overruns", over)

The LDR is a slow sensor (it takes tens of milliseconds to respond), so the 9 Hz wave will be small and rounded, but it should be big enough to see. If the 10 Hz result is not near 1, check that the LDR sees the LED clearly and try 3 seconds more. Print the first 30 values at 10 Hz and look at them: you should see them rise and fall slowly even though the LED is flashing nine times a second.

Solutions

Give each exercise an honest try before opening its solution.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Predict the Noise

Moving average of 16: 14 / √16 = 3.5 counts. EMA with α = 0.1: 14 × √(0.1 / 1.9) = 14 × 0.229 = 3.2 counts. Similar noise reduction; the EMA is cheaper to compute.

Exercise 2: Read the Aliasing

20 Hz sampling has a Nyquist frequency of 10 Hz, and 9 Hz is below it, so the blink is seen correctly: about 9 Hz. (alias_hz(9, 20) gives 9.) The aliasing only appears when the rate drops below 18 Hz.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: Test mean_std

Mean = 55 / 5 = 11. Squared deviations: 1, 1, 0, 4, 4, which sum to 10, so the variance is 2 and the standard deviation is √2 = 1.414.

test_stats.py MicroPython
m, s = mean_std([10, 12, 11, 13, 9]) print(m, round(s, 3)) # 11.0 1.414

Exercise 4: Test the Frequency Counter
test_apparent.py MicroPython
from math import sin, pi def tone(f, hz, seconds=3): return [2000 + 500 * sin(2 * pi * f * k / hz) for k in range(int(hz * seconds))] print(round(apparent_hz(tone(9, 100), 100), 1)) # 9.0 print(round(apparent_hz(tone(9, 10), 10), 1)) # 1.0

The two outputs reproduce the experiment exactly: the same 9 Hz tone reads as 9 Hz when sampled at 100 Hz and as 1 Hz when sampled at 10 Hz. If these tests pass but your hardware result is wrong, the problem is in the circuit or the light path, not the code.
