Lab 4 gives your robot a body that moves and a Decider that closes a loop through the world. You mount two light sensors on a servo, so that the robot can turn its head, and program it to find a light, turn to face it, and keep facing it as the light moves. The Decider has two layers: a state machine that chooses a behaviour (search, track, hold) and a PID controller that does the fine aiming. On the way you use almost everything from the first four weeks: dividers and the ADC, filtering, PWM and servo power, the non-blocking loop, the network on-ramp and sampling theory. This is the robot that Lab Practical 1 tests.

What You Will Build

A light-seeking turret. Two light-dependent resistors (the robot's “eyes”) sit side by side on a short arm fixed to the servo horn, with a card fin between them. When the light is straight ahead both eyes see the same brightness. When the light is off to one side, the fin shades the far eye, and the difference between the two readings tells the robot which way to turn and roughly how far. The robot turns until the eyes balance. Move the flashlight and the head follows; switch it off and the robot sweeps its head until it finds the light again.

The turret from above. The arm on the servo horn carries two LDRs with a card fin between them. Here the turret points right of the light, so the fin shades the right eye: L reads brighter, the balance is positive, and the robot must turn left (toward larger angles, in this drawing) to centre the light. The balance is a synthetic sensor: neither LDR measures direction, but their combination does.

On the robot base, the turret sits at the front edge, its servo powered from the battery pack through the bottom rail, its eyes wired back to the two dividers you built in Lab 2 (the LDR and the thermistor come out; the 10 kΩ resistors stay):

Your robot at the end of this lab: Stage 1 of the course. The parts added in Lab 4 carry the L4 badge: the SG90 servo, the card arm with the twin eyes and fin, the knob, the 4 × AA pack and the 470 µF capacitor across the servo rail.

As always, design the agent before you wire or code it. Compared with Lab 1, the Perceptor now produces numbers rather than events, Memory holds things the PID and the search need, and the Actuator moves a servo as well as the status LEDs.

The turret as a SAM agent. The Perceptor hides the two LDRs, their mismatch and the room light behind three clean percepts. The Decider is now two layers. Publishing telemetry is an action, so it belongs to the Actuator, and it is a side branch: it reports on the loop and never changes it. The physical loop closes through the environment: turning the head is what changes the balance.

Step 1. Build and Wire

Mount the servo on the front edge of the base, horn up, with hot glue or a small bracket. Cut a card arm about 6 cm long, fix it to the horn, and mount the two LDRs at its end about 3 cm apart, each tilted about 20° outward, with a card fin about 4 cm long standing between them. Run their leads back to the Perceptor zone with enough slack for the head to turn from 20° to 160° without pulling.

The Lab 4 wire list. The servo has its own power, as in the PWM lesson: a servo starting or stalling can pull enough current to reset the Pico if it shares the 3V3 or USB supply. The grounds must be joined or the pulse has no reference. The Lab 1 parts stay where they are.

Test in stages before running the full program. First, in the REPL, move the servo to 20°, 90° and 160° and check that nothing binds. Second, print both LDR readings while you shine the flashlight from the left and then the right: the eye on the lit side must read higher. Third, the sign test: point the flashlight from the side the head moves toward when the angle increases, and check that the balance from eyes.sense() goes negative. If it goes positive, swap the two eye pins in Eyes(left=27, right=26). A wrong sign is the commonest cause of a turret that turns away from the light and pins itself against one end.

Step 2. The Decider: Behaviours on Top, PID Underneath

A PID controller is excellent at one thing: driving an error to zero when there is an error to measure. It has no idea what to do when the light is not there at all, or how to tell “aimed” from “almost aimed”. Those are decisions about which behaviour to run, and a state machine handles them. This layering, a discrete chooser on top and continuous controllers underneath, is how most real robots are built, and it is where Unit 2 picks up.

The turret's behaviour layer. SEARCH sweeps the head, starting toward the side where Memory says the light was last seen. TRACK and LOCKED both run the PID; LOCKED only means the aim has been within 0.05 of the target for a full second, which is what the status LEDs and the evt/lock event report. Pressing A in any active state returns to IDLE.

# turret.py for Lab 4: a light-seeking turret as a Situated Agent import net from machine import Pin, PWM, ADC from time import ticks_ms, ticks_diff, sleep_ms from button import Button from pid import PID from every import Every from filters import EMA # ---------------- PERCEPTOR: two eyes, a knob, a button ---------------- class Eyes: def __init__(self, left=26, right=27, knob=28, arm_pin=6): self.l, self.r, self.knob = ADC(left), ADC(right), ADC(knob) self.arm = Button(arm_pin) self.gain = 1.0 # corrects the right eye to match the left self.ambient = 1.0 # room-light level, for brightness self.f = EMA(0.5) def raw(self, n=4): l = sum(self.l.read_u16() for _ in range(n)) / n r = sum(self.r.read_u16() for _ in range(n)) / n * self.gain return l, r def calibrate(self): """Room light only, head straight: match the eyes, remember the room level.""" l, r = self.raw(64) if min(l, r) < 2000: raise ValueError("an eye reads almost zero: check its divider") self.gain = l / r self.ambient = l def sense(self): l, r = self.raw() total = max(l + r, 1) return { "balance": self.f.update((l - r) / total), # > 0: light on the left eye's side "bright": total / (2 * self.ambient), # 1.0 = room light "aim": (self.knob.read_u16() / 65535 - 0.5) * 0.4, # target balance, -0.2 to 0.2 "arm": self.arm.pressed(), } # ---------------- MEMORY ---------------- class Memory: def __init__(self, now): self.state, self.entered_ms = 'IDLE', now self.angle = 90.0 # where the head is (as commanded) self.sweep_dir = 1 # last side the light was seen: +1 or -1 self.good_ms = 0 # how long the aim has been on target self.dark_ms = 0 # how long there has been no light # ---------------- DECIDER: behaviours on top, PID underneath ---------------- FOUND, LOST_MS = 1.25, 500 # "bright" means 25 % above room light LOCK_BAND, LOCK_MS = 0.05, 1000 LO, HI, SWEEP = 20, 160, 60 # head limits (degrees), sweep speed (deg/s) class Decider: def __init__(self, kp=0.5, ki=0.0, kd=0.0): self.pid = PID(kp, ki, kd, -1.0, 1.0) # start P-only; tune in Step 5 def decide(self, p, m, now, dt): ms = int(dt * 1000) s = m.state m.dark_ms = m.dark_ms + ms if p["bright"] < FOUND else 0 # 1. choose the behaviour if p["arm"]: s = 'SEARCH' if s == 'IDLE' else 'IDLE' elif s == 'SEARCH' and p["bright"] >= FOUND: s = 'TRACK' self.pid.i, self.pid.prev = (m.angle - 90) / 80, None # start from where we are elif s in ('TRACK', 'LOCKED') and m.dark_ms >= LOST_MS: s = 'SEARCH' # 2. run it if s == 'IDLE': target = 90 elif s == 'SEARCH': target = m.angle + m.sweep_dir * SWEEP * dt if not LO <= target <= HI: m.sweep_dir = -m.sweep_dir # bounce off the end stops else: # TRACK or LOCKED u = self.pid.update(p["aim"], p["balance"], dt) target = 90 + 80 * u if p["bright"] >= FOUND: # remember where the light is m.sweep_dir = 1 if p["balance"] < p["aim"] else -1 on = abs(p["aim"] - p["balance"]) < LOCK_BAND m.good_ms = m.good_ms + ms if on else 0 s = 'LOCKED' if m.good_ms >= LOCK_MS else 'TRACK' if s != m.state: m.state, m.entered_ms = s, now if s in ('IDLE', 'SEARCH'): m.good_ms = 0 m.angle = max(LO, min(HI, target)) return m.state, m.angle # ---------------- ACTUATOR: servo, LEDs, and the network voice ---------------- def leds_for(state, now_ms): """(red, green) for each state; blinking by the clock, as in Lab 1.""" if state == 'IDLE': return (0, (now_ms // 500) % 2) # slow green blink if state == 'SEARCH': return (0, (now_ms // 125) % 2) # fast green blink if state == 'TRACK': return (0, 1) # green solid return (1, 1) # LOCKED: both solid class Actuator: def __init__(self, servo_pin=14, red_pin=8, green_pin=9): self.servo = PWM(Pin(servo_pin)) self.servo.freq(50) self.red, self.green = Pin(red_pin, Pin.OUT), Pin(green_pin, Pin.OUT) def act(self, state, angle, now): self.servo.duty_ns(1_000_000 + int(angle / 180 * 1_000_000)) r, g = leds_for(state, now) self.red.value(r) self.green.value(g) def report(self, state, p, angle): # publishing is an action too net.publish("tele/turret", {"st": state, "pv": round(p["balance"], 3), "sp": round(p["aim"], 3), "ang": round(angle, 1)}) def event(self, locked, angle): net.publish("evt/lock", {"locked": locked, "ang": round(angle)}) def off(self): self.servo.duty_u16(0) # no pulses: the servo goes limp self.red.off(); self.green.off() # ---------------- THE AGENT LOOP ---------------- eyes, decider, act = Eyes(), Decider(), Actuator() memory = Memory(ticks_ms()) act.act('IDLE', 90, ticks_ms()) sleep_ms(800) # let the head reach the centre eyes.calibrate() control, report = Every(50), Every(200) # 20 Hz control, 5 Hz telemetry last = ticks_ms() p, angle = eyes.sense(), 90.0 try: while True: if control.due(): now = ticks_ms() dt = ticks_diff(now, last) / 1000 last = now p = eyes.sense() # observe before = memory.state state, angle = decider.decide(p, memory, now, dt) # decide act.act(state, angle, now) # act if (state == 'LOCKED') != (before == 'LOCKED'): act.event(state == 'LOCKED', angle) if report.due(): act.report(memory.state, p, angle) finally: act.off() # whatever happens, stop driving

Three details are worth a second look. First, the PID's output range is −1 to 1 and it is mapped to 10° to 170° around the centre (then clamped to the safe 20° to 160°); with the light 30° off centre, the PID must hold an output of about 0.37 to keep the head there. Second, on entering TRACK the integral is preset to the current head position, so the head does not jump back to the centre when the PID takes over from the sweep (a bumpless transfer). Third, nothing in the loop waits: net.publish queues and returns, as in Lab 3, so a network failure cannot stall the aim. The try/finally stops the servo pulses if the program stops for any reason.

Step 4. Measure the Plant

Before closing the loop, measure what you are controlling. Fix the flashlight about 50 cm in front of the robot, put the robot in IDLE, and from the REPL step the head from 30° to 150° in 10° steps, waiting half a second at each and recording the balance. Plot balance against head angle. You should get an S-shaped curve like the one below: nearly straight within about 20° of the light, then flattening as one eye falls fully into the fin's shadow. The slope in the straight part is the sensitivity of your synthetic sensor, and the flat ends are where the PID has very little to work with. Also time the eyes: block the flashlight with a card and see how long the balance takes to settle. LDRs are slow (tens of milliseconds or more), and that delay limits how hard you can push the gains.

The shape to expect from Step 4 (a model curve, not data: your fin, LDR spacing and flashlight will change the slope and the width). Near the light the balance changes steadily with angle, which is what a PID needs. Far from the light it saturates near ±1, so a large error and a very large error look the same. That flat region is one reason the turret needs SEARCH as well as PID.

Step 5. Tune the Loop

The tuning routine. Change one gain at a time, give the loop a few seconds, and write each result in your table. Test 5 is new compared with a plain PID loop: it checks that the behaviour layer and Memory work, because the PID alone would simply drive the head to an end stop when the light disappears.

Test 4 is also the windup test from the PID lesson. If you sweep the light faster than the head can follow, the PID's output saturates at ±1 and an unclamped integral would keep growing, so the head would overshoot badly when it caught up. Ours does not, because the PID class stops integrating while saturated. Confirm that by watching the catch-up.

Step 6. What to Expect

A simulated run of the turret (servo with a 0.1 s lag and a 400°/s speed limit, LDR balance with a 60 ms lag and the S-curve above). The light starts 30° left of centre and jumps 60° to the right at 8 s. Your hardware will be less tidy, so compare shapes, not exact numbers.

Numbers from the simulation, for comparison with yours: P alone leaves the head about 12° short of the light, on both sides. Adding ki = 1 removes the gap; the head is within 3° of the light 0.9 s after starting and 1.6 s after the light moves, without overshoot. With kp = 2 and ki = 4 the loop is too hot: it keeps swinging back and forth across the light (by up to 20° at first) and never settles in the eight seconds shown, and slower real LDRs make that worse. Notice that the PI controller holds the head 30° off centre with zero error. The integral term is doing the remembering: it is the part of the Decider's Memory that knows where the light has been.

# analyze.py: run on your laptop. mosquitto_sub -v -t "lab/USER/+/#" > turret.txt import json def load(path, kind="tele/turret"): """Read mosquitto_sub -v lines: '<topic> <json>'. Returns a list of dicts with t in seconds.""" rows = [] for line in open(path): topic, _, payload = line.partition(" ") if topic.endswith(kind): d = json.loads(payload) d["t"] = d["t"] / 1000 rows.append(d) return rows def settle_time(rows, t0, t1, band=0.05): """Seconds after t0 until |sp - pv| stays inside band up to t1 (None if never).""" seg = [r for r in rows if t0 <= r["t"] < t1] for i, r in enumerate(seg): if all(abs(q["sp"] - q["pv"]) < band for q in seg[i:]): return round(r["t"] - t0, 2) return None def peak_error(rows, t0, t1): return round(max(abs(r["sp"] - r["pv"]) for r in rows if t0 <= r["t"] < t1), 3) def time_in_states(rows): """Seconds spent in each behaviour, from consecutive telemetry samples.""" out = {} for a, b in zip(rows, rows[1:]): out[a["st"]] = round(out.get(a["st"], 0) + b["t"] - a["t"], 1) return out

The script sees what the broker received: five samples a second, while the Decider runs twenty. That matters. In the simulation with kp = 1 and ki = 2, the head overshoots the moved light by about 3.6°, but the 5 Hz telemetry shows no overshoot at all, because the peak falls between two reports. This is the sampling lesson again, applied to your own measurement, and it is the reason the turret also publishes evt/lock: an event is sent at the moment something happens, instead of being left for a fixed-rate sample to catch. time_in_states is your first look at behaviour from outside the robot. In Unit 2 the hub will use exactly this kind of summary to know what every robot is doing.

Solutions

Give each exercise an honest try before opening its solution.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Read the Eyes

Balance = (30 000 − 20 000) / 50 000 = 0.2. Brightness = 50 000 / (2 × 21 000) = 1.19, which is below 1.25, so no target yet: the robot stays in SEARCH even though one eye is lit. A positive balance means the light is on the left eye's side, so once tracking, the head must turn toward the left eye (toward larger angles if you wired it as in the sign test). The brightness rule stops the turret from “tracking” a sunny window or the edge of a desk lamp.

Exercise 2: The Cost of Telemetry

One message is 22 + 70 + 5 = 97 bytes; at 5 Hz that is 485 B/s. The Decider runs at 20 Hz, so there are 4 Decider updates per message, and only the latest one is reported.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: Why P Alone Falls Short

u = kp × error, so error = 0.375 / 0.5 = 0.75 would be needed. But the balance is only 0.75 when tanh(offset / 25) = 0.75, i.e. offset = 25 × atanh(0.75) ≈ 24°. The head cannot settle at 24° short, because at that angle it would be pointing at 96°, needing u ≈ 0.08, not 0.375. The real answer is where the two meet: u = 0.5 × tanh(x / 25) and u = (120 − x − 90) / 80, which gives x ≈ 12°, matching the simulation's 12° gap. Doubling kp to 1 shrinks it to about 7°. It never reaches zero for finite kp, because a P controller needs an error to produce any output; the integral term supplies the 0.375 by itself once the error is gone.
