A processor can only work with numbers, so every sensor reading starts by turning a voltage into a count. This lesson covers how the Pico’s ADC does it, what resolution and quantization mean, and how to turn counts back into volts, resistance and real units, with the two sensors you will use in Lab 2.

SAM lens. This is the inside of the Perceptor: the transducer, the divider that conditions it, the converter, and the scaling that turns a count into a percept with units. A Decider should never see a raw count.

Part 1. From Voltage to a Number

The world is analog: light, temperature, tilt and distance vary smoothly. A processor only handles numbers. An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) measures a voltage and reports it as a whole number. Almost every sensor in the course is read this way, either directly (a light sensor) or through an I2C chip that contains its own converter.

Most simple sensors are resistive: their resistance changes with what they sense. A resistance cannot be read directly, so it is turned into a voltage with a voltage divider (two resistors in series; the voltage between them depends on the ratio), and the ADC reads that voltage.

The path from the physical world to a number in your program. Each stage can add error, and each is something you can reason about: the sensor, the divider, the converter and the scaling in code.

Part 2. The Pico’s ADC

The RP2040 has a 12-bit ADC, which divides the 0 to 3.3 V range into 2^12 = 4096 steps. It has just three external inputs, GP26, GP27 and GP28 (called ADC0, ADC1, ADC2), plus the internal temperature sensor on channel 4 that you used in the first lesson. Other pins are digital-only.

adc_read.py MicroPython
from machine import ADC from time import sleep_ms ldr = ADC(26) # GP26 = ADC0 (ADC(0) means the same thing) while True: raw = ldr.read_u16() # 0..65535: MicroPython scales the 12-bit result up by 16 counts = raw >> 4 # back to the true 0..4095 hardware counts volts = raw * 3.3 / 65535 print(raw, counts, round(volts, 3)) sleep_ms(200)

read_u16() always returns a 16-bit number so that code is the same on every board, whatever its real ADC resolution. On the Pico the low four bits are padding, so only multiples of 16 appear. The reference for the scale is the 3.3 V supply itself, so the reading is a ratio to that supply: if the supply wobbles by 1 %, every reading shifts by 1 %.

Part 3. Quantization

A converter cannot report every voltage; it can only report one of its 4096 levels. Each reading is rounded to the nearest level. That rounding is quantization, and the gap between levels is the step size (or LSB, least significant bit): 3.3 V / 4096 = 0.806 mV per count for the Pico.

Quantization, shown with a deliberately coarse 3-bit converter (8 levels). The ADC reports the staircase, not the smooth curve. The largest error is half a step. The Pico’s 12-bit converter has 512 times as many levels, so its steps are far finer, but the idea is identical.
Bits, levels and step size. Each extra bit doubles the levels and halves the step. More bits does not by itself give a better measurement: noise and the quality of the converter often matter more than the step.

Quantization error is at most half a step, 0.4 mV for the Pico. In practice the Pico’s ADC is a little worse than its 12 bits suggest: its datasheet quotes about 8.7 effective bits, because of small non-uniformities and noise, so the last bit or two of a reading will jitter. That is normal, and is what filtering (later this week) is for.

Part 4. Turning Counts into Units

A reading in counts is not very useful by itself. Convert it step by step: counts to volts, volts to resistance, resistance to the physical quantity. Two dividers cover our two sensors.

The two dividers used in Lab 2. In the LDR circuit light lowers the LDR’s resistance, which raises the voltage. In the thermistor circuit the fixed resistor is on top, so a warmer (lower resistance) thermistor lowers the voltage. Which resistor is on top decides which way the signal goes.
convert.py MicroPython
from math import log R_FIXED = 10_000 # the 10 kOhm resistor in each divider def counts_to_volts(raw): return raw * 3.3 / 65535 def ldr_ohms(raw): """LDR on top, fixed resistor below: R_ldr = R * (3.3 - V) / V.""" v = counts_to_volts(raw) return R_FIXED * (3.3 - v) / v def ntc_celsius(raw, r0=10_000, beta=3950): """Thermistor below the fixed resistor. Beta equation, 25 C reference.""" v = counts_to_volts(raw) r = R_FIXED * v / (3.3 - v) # thermistor resistance inv_t = 1 / 298.15 + log(r / r0) / beta return 1 / inv_t - 273.15 print(round(ldr_ohms(32768))) # about 10000: LDR = 10 kOhm at mid-scale print(round(ntc_celsius(32768), 1)) # 25.0: equal resistances means 25 C

The thermistor numbers r0 = 10 kΩ at 25 °C and beta = 3950 are common values for the parts in starter kits but are not guaranteed: check the part marking or the kit’s documentation, and calibrate against a thermometer in the lab. A wrong beta gives a smooth, plausible and wrong temperature.

Notice what the quantization step means in the real world. Near 25 °C one count of this thermistor circuit is about 0.022 °C, so the converter is not what limits your temperature accuracy. The thermistor’s tolerance, its beta value and self-heating are much larger errors. Know which error dominates before you worry about the others.

The results are about 15.2 °C, 25.0 °C and 37.9 °C. Smaller raw values mean a lower voltage, which means a lower thermistor resistance, which means a warmer temperature. In this wiring the reading falls as it warms. If your numbers rise with temperature, your divider is wired the other way round.

Paper Practice: Answers

Recall

P1. Levels and Step

2^12 = 4096 levels; 3.3 V / 4096 = 0.806 mV per count.

P2. Why Multiples of 16

It always returns a 16-bit number, but the Pico's ADC is 12-bit; the four lowest bits are padding (zero), so the value is the 12-bit count times 16.

P3. Analog Inputs

GP26 = ADC0, GP27 = ADC1, GP28 = ADC2; the temperature sensor is channel 4.

P4. Effective Bits

Noise and non-uniformity mean only about 8 to 9 bits are trustworthy; the last bit or two jitter. Roughly 2^8.7 ≈ 416 distinguishable levels, or about 8 mV of meaningful resolution.

Apply

P5. Counts to Volts

20000 × 3.3 / 65535 = 1.007 V. The 12-bit count is 20000 / 16 = 1250.

P6. Quantization Error

Half a step: 0.403 mV for 12 bits. For 3 bits: 3.3 / 8 = 0.4125 V per step, so up to 0.206 V.

P7. Divider Voltage

V = 3.3 × 10 / (R + 10): 2 kΩ gives 2.75 V; 50 kΩ gives 0.55 V. 2 kΩ is brighter (light lowers an LDR's resistance).

P8. Volts to Resistance

R = 10 kΩ × (3.3 / 1.65 − 1) = 10 kΩ.

P9. Thermistor

R = 10 kΩ × 1.20 / (3.3 − 1.20) = 5.71 kΩ. 1/T = 1/298.15 + ln(0.5714)/3950 = 0.0033540 − 0.0001417 = 0.0032123, so T = 311.3 K = 38.1 °C. Warmer than 25 °C, as a lower resistance should be.

Analyze and Design

P10. Best Fixed Resistor

1 kΩ: 1.65 V to 0.033 V, swing 1.62 V. 10 kΩ: 3.00 V to 0.30 V, swing 2.70 V. 100 kΩ: 3.27 V to 1.65 V, swing 1.62 V. 10 kΩ is best: a fixed resistor near the geometric mean of the sensor's range (√(1 k × 100 k) = 10 kΩ) gives the largest swing.

P11. Supply Wobble

The count does not change: the divider output and the reference both scale by the same factor, so the reading is ratiometric. But the computed voltage is wrong by about 3 % (the code assumes 3.3 V). Resistance computed from the ratio is still correct, which is why converting counts straight to resistance is robust.

P12. Which Error Dominates

Against: quantization is already far smaller than the other errors (thermistor tolerance, uncertainty in β, self-heating, calibration), which are typically tenths of a degree or more. Calibrating against a reference thermometer would help much more than more bits.
