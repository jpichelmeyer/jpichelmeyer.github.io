Before the Pico shows up, this lesson gives you the electronics that every later lesson takes for granted: what voltage, current and resistance are, how to read a circuit diagram, how a breadboard is wired, and how to check a circuit with a multimeter and an oscilloscope. Everything here runs on a 4 × AA battery pack. No microcontroller is needed, and by the end you will have built several working circuits and proved with measurements that they work.

SAM lens. A Perceptor and an Actuator are circuits before they are code. A button, a light sensor and an LED are all parts in a loop, and when something does not work the first question is almost always electrical. The multimeter and the oscilloscope are how you ask it.

Part 1. Voltage, Current and Resistance

A circuit is a loop. Charge flows round it, pushed by a source (here, a battery) and slowed by each part it passes through. Three quantities describe what is going on.

  • Voltage (V), in volts. The push. Voltage is always measured between two points. When someone says the voltage "at" a point, they mean between that point and ground, the point we agree to call 0 V.
  • Current (I), in amps (A). How much charge passes a point each second. In this course the numbers are small, so you will mostly see milliamps: 1 mA = 0.001 A. By convention current flows from the + side of the source, round the loop, and back to the − side.
  • Resistance (R), in ohms (Ω). How hard it is to push current through something. 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.
The same idea twice. In the water loop the pump supplies pressure, the narrow pipe resists the flow, and the amount of water passing each second is the flow. In the wire loop the battery supplies voltage, the resistor resists, and the charge passing each second is the current.

Ohm's law ties the three together: V = I × R. Rearranged, I = V / R and R = V / I. Double the resistance and the same voltage gives half the current. A resistor with 2.8 V across it and 330 Ω of resistance carries 2.8 / 330 = 0.0085 A, which is 8.5 mA.

Two more facts are worth having now. First, in a series loop the same current flows through every part, and the voltages across the parts add up to the source voltage. Second, power is P = V × I, in watts. Power turns into heat, and the little resistors in your kit are rated for 1/4 W (250 mW). That 330 Ω resistor dissipates 2.8 V × 8.5 mA = 24 mW, nowhere near its limit.

Your power source. The pack holds four rechargeable AA cells in series. Each cell is rated 1.2 V, so the pack is nominally 4.8 V. Fresh off the charger it can read up to about 5.6 V, and as it runs down it falls toward 4.4 V and below. Alkaline cells are 1.5 V each, which would make 6 V. The lesson is to measure the pack rather than trust the label, and to use the measured value in your calculations.

A pack of these cells can deliver an ampere or more. That is the reason for the one rule that applies to every circuit in this lesson: every loop needs something in it that limits the current. If you connect the + side to the − side with a bare wire (a short circuit), nothing limits the current, and the wire, the pack and your fingers get warm very quickly. Connect the power last, check the loop before you do, and disconnect it first when something looks wrong.

Part 2. Reading a Circuit Diagram

A schematic is a drawing of what connects to what, using one symbol for each kind of part. Where things sit on the page does not matter; the lines between them do. You will read schematics in every lesson and lab from here on, so this part is the alphabet. Skim the five sheets now and come back to them as a reference.

The first sheet is the source and its reference. A battery cell is a long thin plate (+) and a short thick plate (−); a pack is several in a row, and the cell voltages add. A supply rail is a named voltage, drawn as a bar with a label such as 3V3 or 5V. And there are ground symbols. Every voltage in a circuit is measured against one agreed point, and ground marks it. The three-line symbol is used for every ground in this course, and all the grounds in one drawing are the same wire. You will meet the other two on data sheets: the triangle for a quiet signal ground (the Pico has a pin called AGND that serves the analog inputs) and the hatched one for a metal chassis.

Sources and grounds. The pack on your robot is the four-cell symbol. Wherever you see a ground symbol, that point is at 0 V, and it is connected to every other ground symbol on the drawing.

The second sheet holds the parts that resist or store. A resistor limits current. A potentiometer is a resistor with a sliding contact (the wiper): turn the knob and the wiper picks off a different fraction of the resistance. The photoresistor (LDR) and the thermistor are resistors whose value changes with light and with temperature, which is what makes them sensors. A capacitor stores a little charge and evens out sudden changes; the electrolytic type has a + leg that must go to the higher voltage. An inductor is a coil. You will rarely wire one yourself, but motors and relay coils behave like one, and that matters in the Switching Loads lesson.

Resistors, capacitors and coils. The same resistor has two drawings: the zigzag is used throughout this course. The arrows on the LDR show light arriving.

The third sheet holds the parts that steer. A diode passes current in one direction only, toward its bar. An LED is a diode that gives off light. A transistor has three terminals, and a small current into the base lets a much larger current flow from collector to emitter, which makes it an electrically controlled switch. Your kit has an NPN (S8050) and a PNP (S8550); the pin order differs between makers, so check the data sheet for the part in your hand before plugging it in. A switch stays where you leave it, a pushbutton is closed only while you press it, and a relay closes a switch using a coil.

Diodes, transistors and switches. Anything with a triangle, an arrow or a plus sign has a direction. Resistors, switches and ordinary capacitors can go in either way round.

Not every part has a standard symbol. A servo, a sensor module, a display and the Pico itself are drawn as labelled boxes, and the only thing that matters is the name on each pin. The same sheet shows the symbols for the three meters, which you will use shortly.

Loads, modules and meters. When a part has no symbol, draw a box and write the pin names inside, exactly as printed on the part.

Last come wires. A dot where two lines cross means they are connected. Two lines crossing with no dot are not. Two wire ends with the same name are one wire, even though no line joins them, which keeps big drawings readable. The oscilloscope probe has its own symbol: a tip on the point you want to see, and a clip on ground.

Wires and probes. Always check for the dot.

A schematic and a breadboard describe the same circuit in two ways. The schematic shows what is connected; the breadboard shows where each part sits. Moving from one to the other is a skill you will practise in every lab.

Part 3. Resistors in Practice

Resistors are made in standard values: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82, and those numbers times 10, 100, 1000 and so on. You rarely get exactly the value you calculate, so when a resistor is protecting something you pick the next standard value up. The value is printed as coloured bands.

The resistor colour code. Read from the end that has the bands closer together. The first two bands are digits, the third says how many zeros to add, and the last is the tolerance.

When you need a value you do not have, you can build it. Resistors in series add: the current is shared and the voltage is split. Resistors in parallel give a smaller resistance, because the current has two paths. And two resistors in series with a tap between them form a voltage divider, which is how a sensor whose resistance changes becomes a voltage that changes. You will see the divider again and again: the light sensor and thermistor in the ADC lesson are both one half of a divider.

Series, parallel and the divider. In the divider, the output is the fraction R2 / (R1 + R2) of the input. Equal resistors give half. If R2 is a sensor, Vout follows the sensor.

A worked example. Two 10 kΩ resistors across the 4.8 V pack give 4.8 × 10 000 / 20 000 = 2.4 V at the tap. Replace the lower one with a 1 kΩ resistor and the tap drops to 4.8 × 1000 / 11 000 = 0.44 V. The tap voltage depends only on the ratio, not on the actual sizes, although the sizes decide how much current the divider draws.

Part 4. A Breadboard Is Just Hidden Wires

Inside a breadboard. Each short column of five holes is connected underneath, so two parts plugged into the same column are joined. The resistor here connects strip A to strip B; anything else in either column is connected to it too. The gap in the middle separates the two halves.

The same idea runs along the long power rails at the edges of the board: each rail is a single wire the length of the board, usually marked with a red line (+) and a blue line (−). On many full-size boards the rail is cut in the middle, so the two halves are not connected until you add a jumper; if a rail seems dead halfway along, that is the reason. The holes are 0.1 inch apart. Use thin solid-core wire and part leads that slide in easily, and never force a thick lead into a hole, because it stretches the contact and makes the board unreliable.

Build in neat, short wires, and always connect a common ground first: it is the reference every other voltage is measured against. Keep one rail for + and the other for ground (−), and label them with tape if it helps. The multimeter in continuity mode (below) is the quickest way to prove that two holes really are connected.

Part 5. Your First Circuit: An LED on the Pack

An LED has two legs and a direction. Current must enter at the anode (+) and leave at the cathode (−). Once it is lit, an LED holds a nearly fixed voltage across itself, called its forward voltage: about 2.0 V for red, about 2.1 V for green, and about 3 V for blue or white. Because that voltage barely changes with current, an LED has no useful resistance of its own, and without something to limit the current it would draw as much as the pack can give and burn out in a moment. So an LED always has a series resistor.

An LED in real life and as a symbol. If an LED does not light, turn it around before you do anything else.

Choosing the resistor takes six steps. Do them in order, with numbers, every time.

  1. Source voltage. Measure the pack. We will use 4.8 V.
  2. LED forward voltage. Red is about 2.0 V.
  3. Current you want. A red LED is clearly visible at 5 to 10 mA, and its limit is about 20 mA. Choose 10 mA.
  4. Resistor. The resistor must drop everything the LED does not: R = (4.8 V − 2.0 V) / 10 mA = 2.8 / 0.010 = 280 Ω.
  5. Standard value, rounded up. 280 Ω is not a standard value; the next one up that you have is 330 Ω.
  6. Check the real current. I = 2.8 V / 330 Ω = 8.5 mA. With a freshly charged pack at 5.4 V the current is (5.4 − 2.0) / 330 = 10.3 mA, still safe. The resistor dissipates 2.8 V × 8.5 mA = 24 mW, far below 250 mW.
The finished circuit with every number on it. The drop across the resistor and the drop across the LED add up to the pack voltage, and the same current flows through both.

Now build it on the breadboard. Leave the pack disconnected from the rails until the very end, so nothing is live while your hands are in the circuit.

The same circuit on a breadboard, viewed from above. The orange outlines mark the three strips that matter. Red jumper: + rail to column 6. Resistor: column 6 to column 10. The LED shares column 10 with the resistor (long leg, anode) and has its short leg in column 11, where the black jumper returns to the rail.
  1. Push the pack's red lead into the + rail and its black lead into the − rail. Leave the pack switch off, or one lead out, for now.
  2. Place the red jumper from the + rail to column 6.
  3. Place the 330 Ω resistor from column 6 to column 10.
  4. Place the LED with its long leg in column 10 and its short leg in column 11.
  5. Place the black jumper from column 11 to the − rail. Last, connect the pack. The LED lights.

If it does not light, work through this list: the LED is the wrong way round (turn it); a leg is in the wrong column (check the strip each part is in); a jumper is not pushed all the way in; the pack is flat (measure it); the resistor is much too large (check the bands). If it lights very brightly and then dies, the resistor was missing or far too small.

Notice what this circuit is: a source, a part that limits the current, and an indicator that lets you see the result. In the Circuits and GPIO lesson a Pico pin takes the place of the pack as the source, and the loop stays exactly the same.

Part 6. The Multimeter

A multimeter is a voltmeter, an ohmmeter and an ammeter in one box, selected with the dial. It gives you one number at a time. The black lead always goes in the COM jack. The red lead goes in the jack that matches the measurement: the one marked V Ω for volts, ohms and continuity, and the one marked mA (or 10 A) for current.

A typical multimeter. Your model may look different, but it has the same ideas: a dial, a display, a common jack and a few jacks for the red lead.

Each measurement connects the meter differently, and getting the connection wrong is the main way people make a meter show nonsense (or blow its fuse).

  • Volts (DC). Put the meter across the part, in parallel, with the circuit running. Red lead on the more positive point. A voltmeter has a very high resistance (millions of ohms), so it barely disturbs the circuit. If the display shows a minus sign, the leads are the wrong way round, which is harmless.
  • Ohms. Measure a resistor by itself with the power off. If it is still in a circuit, other paths give a wrong answer, so lift one leg out of the board. Hold the part by its body, because touching both leads adds your own resistance to large readings. The meter shows OL (over limit) when the resistance is too large for the range, or the leads are open.
  • Continuity. The dial position with a sound-wave symbol. The meter beeps when the resistance between its leads is very small (typically under about 50 Ω). Use it to check that a wire is not broken and that two breadboard holes really are connected. Power must be off.
  • Diode test. Shows the forward voltage of a diode or LED. A red LED reads about 1.7 to 2 V in the forward direction, and may glow faintly; reversed it reads OL. This is also a quick way to find which leg is the anode.
  • Current. This is different from the others: you must break the loop and put the meter in the gap, in series, so that the whole current passes through it. Move the red lead to the mA jack (or the 10 A jack if you have no idea how much current to expect) and set the dial to match. An ammeter has almost no resistance, so if you put it across the pack or across a part you have made a short circuit; the meter's fuse blows, and until you replace it the current ranges stop working. Move the red lead back to the V Ω jack when you finish.
The three connections. Volts: in parallel, circuit on. Ohms: part alone, circuit off. Amps: in series, so the loop is opened and the meter closes it.

A good habit is to predict the reading before you take it. In the LED circuit you expect 4.8 V across the pack, 2.8 V across the resistor, 2.0 V across the LED, and 8.5 mA in the loop. If the meter disagrees by more than about 10 percent, you have found something worth understanding.

Part 7. The Oscilloscope

A multimeter tells you one number. An oscilloscope draws a graph: voltage up the screen, time along it. That matters whenever a voltage changes quickly, because the meter averages it away. The signals that you will look at later in the course (a button bouncing, a PWM wave driving a servo, the pulses of an ultrasonic sensor) are all too fast for a meter and obvious on a scope.

The screen is a grid. Each square is a division. The volts/div knob sets how many volts one division is tall, and the time/div knob sets how long one division is wide. To read a value, count divisions and multiply: a trace 2.4 divisions above the ground marker at 2 V/div is 4.8 V. For a repeating signal, the time for one full cycle is the period T, and the frequency is f = 1 / T.

Three traces. A battery is a flat line. A pressed button does not rise cleanly: the contacts chatter, and the scope shows the bounce that a meter would miss. The last trace is a square wave of the kind many scopes put out for calibration; its period and frequency are read from the grid.

Five things to know before you touch the knobs.

  • Probe. The tip goes on the point you want to see and the ground clip goes on the circuit's ground (the pack −, or the Pico GND later). Never clip it to a point that is not ground: on a bench scope the clip is connected to the wall earth, and clipping it to a live point shorts that point to earth. Most probes have a 1x/10x switch; the channel setting on the scope must match it, or every number will be out by a factor of ten.
  • Coupling. Set it to DC so that you see real voltage levels, not just the changing part.
  • Scale first. Pick a volts/div that makes the signal fill most of the screen without running off it, and a time/div that shows one to a few cycles. Move the ground marker so there is room to see the trace.
  • Trigger. The trigger tells the scope when to start each sweep, so that a repeating signal sits still. Choose the rising edge and a level halfway up the signal. For something that happens once, such as one button press, use single mode: the scope waits and freezes the trace when the event arrives.
  • Practice signal. Most scopes have a small terminal on the front that outputs a square wave (often about 1 kHz) for calibrating probes. Clip the probe there and practise reading amplitude, period and frequency before you try your own circuit.

Part 8. Practice Circuits

These are the exercises for this lesson, and none of them needs a microcontroller. For each one, the rule is the same: build it, predict the readings, measure, and compare. The "It works if" list says how to decide you are done. Do not take its numbers on faith; write down yours, and if they differ, find out why. A circuit that lights up but gives the wrong readings is not finished.

You need: the charged 4 × AA pack, a breadboard, jumper wires, red and green LEDs, resistors (100 Ω, 330 Ω, 10 kΩ, 100 kΩ), a pushbutton, the potentiometer, the LDR, an S8050 transistor, the small DC motor or fan, a multimeter, and the scope for Build 9. Connect the pack last every time.

Build 1. Check the pack

Build 1. The voltmeter across the pack, red lead on the red wire.

Do. Set the meter to DC volts and touch the red lead to the pack's red wire and the black lead to the black wire.

It works if the reading is steady and between about 4.4 V and 5.6 V. A reading near 4.0 V or lower means the pack needs charging. A negative number means the leads are reversed. A reading near zero means a lead is not touching, or the pack is switched off. Write the number down: it is the Vs in every calculation that follows.

Build 2. LED and resistor

Do. Build the circuit of Part 5. Then take four measurements, with the circuit running: the voltage across the resistor, the voltage across the LED, the voltage across the pack, and (with the meter in series) the current.

It works if the LED glows steadily; the resistor reads about 2.6 to 3.0 V; the LED reads about 1.8 to 2.2 V; those two readings add up to the pack voltage within 0.1 V; and the current is within 20 percent of your calculation (about 7 to 10 mA). Also notice the pack voltage with the LED connected and compare it with Build 1: it sags a little under load, which is normal for a battery.

Build 3. A switch in the loop

Build 3. A pushbutton in series with the resistor and the LED.

Do. Add the pushbutton in series with the resistor and the LED, as drawn. Then measure the voltage across the button when it is released and when it is pressed.

It works if the LED is off at rest and on only while you hold the button. Across the button you should read about the full pack voltage when it is released, and close to 0 V when it is pressed. That is a general rule worth remembering: an open switch has the whole supply across it, because no current flows to drop voltage anywhere else, and a closed switch has almost none. If the LED is always on, the button's legs are probably on the same side; turn it a quarter turn.

Build 4. Two LEDs: series and parallel

Build 4. Two red LEDs in series share one resistor and one current. In parallel, each LED has its own resistor and its own current.

Do. Build the series circuit first, with a 100 Ω resistor and two red LEDs. Measure the voltage across each LED and across the resistor, and the current. Pull one LED out and watch. Then build the parallel circuit with a 330 Ω resistor in each branch, measure the current in each branch and the total current, and pull one LED out.

It works if in the series circuit both LEDs glow equally; each reads about 2.0 V and the resistor about 0.8 V, so the three add up to the pack; the current is about 8 mA; and pulling either LED makes both go out, because the loop is broken. In the parallel circuit both glow; each branch carries about 8.5 mA; the total is about 17 mA; and pulling one LED leaves the other lit.

Then think. Try three LEDs in series. Why will they not light? (Add up the forward voltages and compare with the pack.)

Build 5. A dimmer

Build 5. The potentiometer used as a variable resistor, in series with a fixed 330 Ω. The wiper is tied to one end.

Do. First measure the potentiometer with the ohmmeter, across its two outer pins, to find its full resistance. Then build the circuit as drawn and turn the knob slowly. Measure the voltage across the LED at both ends of the travel.

It works if the brightness changes smoothly from nearly dark to bright, and the LED voltage moves a little (about 1.7 V when dim to about 2.0 V when bright). The fixed 330 Ω stays in the loop so that the current can never exceed about 8.5 mA, even when the pot is turned to zero. Leave it out and the pot will happily let the LED burn.

Build 6. A light sensor as a voltage divider

Build 6. The LDR on top, a fixed 10 kΩ below, and the voltmeter across the fixed resistor.

Do. Build the divider as drawn and read the voltage at the tap in room light, then with your hand cupped over the LDR, then with a phone torch close to it. Record the three readings. Then compute the LDR's resistance from each: RLDR = 10 kΩ × (Vs − Vout) / Vout. Finally, take the LDR out of the board and measure it directly with the ohmmeter in the same three conditions.

It works if more light gives a higher voltage and less light a lower one, with at least a 2 to 1 change between bright and covered, and the resistance you calculated agrees with the resistance you measured to within about 20 percent. Getting the same answer by two different routes is the best sign you have understood the circuit.

Build 7. A dark detector with a transistor

Build 7. The LDR and the 100 kΩ resistor make a divider whose tap goes to the base of the S8050. In the dark the tap rises, the transistor turns on, and the LED lights.

Do. Build the circuit as drawn. Check the data sheet for the S8050's pin order before you plug it in. Measure the voltage at the base node (the tap) with the room light on, and with your hand over the LDR.

It works if the LED is off in normal light and comes on when you cover the LDR, and the base voltage reads well below 0.6 V in the light and settles near 0.6 to 0.7 V when the LED is on. It cannot go much higher, because the base-emitter junction is itself a diode and clamps at about 0.7 V. That clamp is how the transistor works: once the base reaches about 0.7 V, a small base current lets a much larger current flow from collector to emitter, through the LED.

If it needs tuning. If the LED never goes off in a bright room, make the top resistor larger by putting two 100 kΩ resistors in series. If it never comes on, make it smaller, with a 10 kΩ. This is the first time you have used a small signal to switch a bigger load, which is exactly what the Switching Loads lesson does with a Pico pin as the small signal.

Build 8. Making something spin

Build 8. A switch, the motor, and (optionally) a diode across the motor with its bar toward +.

Do. Connect the motor (or the small fan) to the pack through a switch, as drawn, and close it. Swap the two motor wires and close it again. If you have a diode, add it across the motor with its bar toward the + side. With the meter in series (use the 10 A jack if you do not know the current), measure the running current.

It works if the motor spins when you close the switch, spins the opposite way when you swap its wires, and draws a steady current that is much larger than an LED's: tens or hundreds of milliamps. Compare it with the 8.5 mA of the LED circuit. This is why a Pico pin, which can supply only a few milliamps, cannot drive a motor directly. A motor draws far more when it is held still, so do not hold the shaft for more than a moment.

What about a servo? A servo is the one part you cannot make spin with a battery alone. It only moves in response to a repeating pulse that tells it where to go, and making that pulse is a job for a microcontroller (the PWM and Servos lesson). What you can do now is prove its power is right: connect the red and brown wires of an SG90 servo to the + and − rails (brown is ground, red is power, orange is the signal, which stays unconnected) and measure about the pack voltage across them. It should stay cool and quiet. Reversing the power to a servo damages it, so check the colours twice.

Build 9. See it on the scope

Build 9. The probe tip is on the node between the button and the 10 kΩ resistor, and the ground clip is on the pack's − side.

Do, part one. Clip the probe to the scope's calibration terminal. Choose a volts/div and time/div that put two or three cycles on the screen (1 V/div and 0.5 ms/div is a good start), and trigger on the rising edge at about half height. Read the amplitude, the period and the frequency from the grid.

Do, part two. Build the circuit as drawn, with the ground clip on the − side of the pack. Set 2 V/div and 1 ms/div, DC coupling, trigger on the rising edge at about 2 V in single mode, and press the button. Measure how long the signal takes to settle.

It works if in part one the amplitude, period and frequency are consistent with each other (f times T is 1), and in part two you capture a trace that rises from about 0 V to about the pack voltage. Some presses show several chatter edges before the signal settles; some are clean. Press a few times and keep the worst one. Write down how long the worst one took to settle. In the Circuits and GPIO lesson you will see why that number decides how long a debounce delay needs to be.

Exercises

Practice problems in three tiers. Do each on paper first, and only then open its solution below. Many problems have more than one correct answer; if yours behaves the same way, it is right even if it looks different.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Pick a Resistor. A green LED drops 2.1 V. From the 4.8 V pack you want about 10 mA through it. What resistor do you calculate, and what current flows with the 330 Ω you have?

Exercise 2: Current and Power in One Resistor. A 4.7 kΩ resistor is across the 4.8 V pack. What current flows, and how much power does the resistor dissipate? What happens to both if you use 470 Ω instead?

Intermediate

Exercise 3: Light Divider. An LDR is 2 kΩ in bright light and 100 kΩ in the dark. It sits on top of a 10 kΩ resistor, across 4.8 V, and you read the voltage across the 10 kΩ. What do you read in each case, and which way does the voltage move as the light fades?

Exercise 4: Two LEDs, One Resistor. A red LED (2.0 V) and a green LED (2.1 V) are in series with a 100 Ω resistor on the 4.8 V pack. What is the current? Then a student adds a third LED of 3.0 V in series. What happens, and why?

Advanced

Exercise 5: The Dark Detector Threshold. In Build 7 the base voltage must reach about 0.6 V before the transistor starts to turn on. With the 100 kΩ top resistor and a 4.8 V supply, at what LDR resistance does that happen? (Ignore the small base current.) What is the threshold if you use 200 kΩ on top, and does the LED then come on in a lighter or a darker room?

Solutions

Give each exercise an honest try before opening its solution.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Pick a Resistor

R = (4.8 − 2.1) / 0.010 = 270 Ω. If you only have 330 Ω, I = 2.7 / 330 = 8.2 mA, which is bright and well inside the safe range.

Exercise 2: Current and Power in One Resistor

I = 4.8 / 4700 = 1.02 mA, and P = V × I = 4.8 × 0.00102 = 4.9 mW. With 470 Ω both go up by a factor of ten: 10.2 mA and 49 mW. That is still far below the 250 mW rating, but the current is already as large as you would put through an LED.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: Light Divider

The 10 kΩ is the lower resistor, so Vout = 4.8 × 10 / (RLDR + 10). In bright light: 4.8 × 10 / 12 = 4.0 V. In the dark: 4.8 × 10 / 110 = 0.44 V. As the light fades the LDR's resistance rises, it takes a larger share of the supply, and the voltage across the fixed resistor falls.

Exercise 4: Two LEDs, One Resistor

The LEDs drop 2.0 + 2.1 = 4.1 V, leaving 0.7 V for the resistor, so I = 0.7 / 100 = 7 mA. With a third LED the three drops add up to 7.1 V, more than the 4.8 V the pack supplies, so there is no voltage left to push the current and none of them lights (at best a faint glow). Series LEDs need a source larger than the sum of their forward voltages.

Advanced

Exercise 5: The Dark Detector Threshold

The base voltage is the divider output with the LDR below: 4.8 × RLDR / (RLDR + 100 kΩ) = 0.6. So RLDR / (RLDR + 100 kΩ) = 0.125, which gives RLDR = 0.125 × 100 kΩ / 0.875 = 14.3 kΩ. The LED comes on when the LDR rises above about 14 kΩ. With 200 kΩ on top the threshold doubles to about 28.6 kΩ, so the LDR must be higher in resistance, which means darker: the LED comes on later as the room dims.

Paper Practice

Short problems for pencil and paper, in three levels: Recall (definitions and facts), Apply (calculations and traces) and Analyze and Design (explain, debug, choose, design). None needs a board or a meter. Answers follow the problems; try each one first.

Recall

P1. Ohm's Law. Write Ohm's law and give the unit of each quantity in it. Which quantity does a resistor in series with an LED limit?

P2. LED Polarity. Which leg of an LED goes toward the positive side, and how do you recognise it?

P3. Series or Parallel. For parts in series, which quantity is the same through all of them? For parts in parallel, which quantity is the same across all of them?

P4. Name the Symbol. Name each symbol and give one use: (a) a zigzag line; (b) a triangle against a bar, with two small arrows pointing away; (c) a stem with three horizontal lines of decreasing length; (d) a circle containing the letter A.

Paper Practice: Answers

Recall

P1. Ohm's Law

V = I × R: volts, amps, ohms. The resistor limits the current.

P2. LED Polarity

The anode, the longer leg (the cathode side usually has a flat on the rim of the LED).

P3. Series or Parallel

In series the current is the same through every part. In parallel the voltage is the same across every part.

P4. Name the Symbol

(a) A resistor: limits current. (b) An LED: gives light when current flows toward the bar. (c) Ground: the 0 V reference. (d) An ammeter: measures current, connected in series.

Apply

P5. LED Current

(4.8 − 2.0) / 220 = 12.7 mA; (4.8 − 2.0) / 330 = 8.5 mA. Both are below the 20 mA limit; 12.7 mA is bright.

P6. Choose a Resistor

R = (4.8 − 2.2) / 0.005 = 520 Ω. 470 Ω gives 5.5 mA and 680 Ω gives 3.8 mA; either is fine, and 680 Ω is the conservative choice (rounding up).

P7. Breadboard Strips

Yes. Rows a to e of one column are one strip, so the resistor's end in column 14 (row b) joins the LED leg in column 14 (row d), and the LED's other leg in column 15 joins the ground wire in column 15. Column 10 → resistor → column 14 → LED → column 15 → ground.

P8. Combine Resistors

The pair in parallel is 500 Ω. In series with 330 Ω the total is 830 Ω. I = 4.8 / 830 = 5.8 mA.

Analyze and Design

P9. Meter in the Wrong Place

An ammeter has almost no resistance, so across the pack it is a short circuit: a very large current flows, the meter's fuse blows (or the leads and pack get hot). They should have opened the loop and put the meter in series, or measured the voltage in volts mode instead.

P10. Dead LED Checklist

(1) Measure the pack voltage: a flat pack explains everything. (2) Polarity: turn the LED around. (3) The loop: the resistor and LED share a strip, and the LED returns to the − rail, with every jumper pushed in (use continuity to check). (4) The resistor value: much too large a resistor starves the LED, and a resistor accidentally placed with both legs in one strip is shorted out.

P11. Open and Closed

Released: about 4.8 V, the full pack voltage, because no current flows, so there is no drop across the resistor or the LED, and everything is across the open switch. Pressed: close to 0 V, because a closed switch is a wire and wires drop almost nothing.

P12. Read the Scope

Amplitude: 3.3 divisions × 1 V/div = 3.3 V. Period: 4 divisions × 0.5 ms = 2 ms. Frequency: 1 / 2 ms = 500 Hz. A 3.3 V amplitude is what a Pico pin gives when it is high.

  4. Say which holes of a breadboard are connected, and build a circuit from a schematic.
  5. Measure volts, ohms and current with a multimeter, and say how the meter is connected for each.
  6. Read amplitude, period and frequency from an oscilloscope screen.
