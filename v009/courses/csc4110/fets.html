Everything so far has sent information. This lesson is about doing something in the physical world: switching a fan, a relay, a strip of LEDs. A Pico pin cannot do that on its own, so you need a switch controlled by a pin, and for that the MOSFET is the standard tool.

Part 1. Why a Pin Cannot Drive a Load

A GPIO pin is built to send a signal, not power. By default it supplies only a few milliamps (the RP2040 lets you configure it up to about 12 mA, but check the datasheet), and the 3.3 V supply on the board is limited too. A small fan, a relay coil or a motor wants hundreds of milliamps and often a higher voltage. Connect one directly and the best case is that nothing happens; the worst is a damaged pin.

The solution is a switch that the pin controls but does not power. A pin turns the switch on and off; the switch carries the load’s current from a separate supply.

Part 2. The MOSFET as a Switch

An N-channel MOSFET is a switch controlled by voltage. When the gate is far enough above the source, current can flow from drain to source; the gate itself draws almost no current.

An N-channel MOSFET has three terminals. Voltage on the gate controls whether current can flow from the drain to the source. Once the gate is a few volts above the source the switch is on; at zero volts it is off. Because the gate is insulated from the channel it draws almost no steady current, which is why a weak pin can control a strong load.

Part 3. The Low-Side Switch

The standard circuit puts the MOSFET between the load and ground, so the load is always connected to the supply and the MOSFET completes the path to ground when told to. Every part has a job:

The standard low-side switch. The Pico drives the gate through a small resistor; a pull-down keeps the load off while the Pico is booting; the load sits between the supply and the drain; a diode protects against inductive spikes.
  • Gate resistor (330 Ω). The gate behaves like a small capacitor, so switching on briefly draws a surge. 3.3 V through 330 Ω limits that to about 10 mA, within what a pin can supply.
  • Pull-down (10 kΩ). Gate to ground. While the Pico is resetting or being reprogrammed its pins are not driving anything, and a floating gate can turn the load on at random. The pull-down guarantees OFF unless the pin says otherwise.
  • Flyback diode. Across the load, explained next. Needed for any coil or motor.
  • Common ground. The Pico’s ground and the load supply’s ground must be connected together; otherwise the gate voltage has no reference and the circuit does nothing useful.

Part 4. The Flyback Diode

A coil or motor stores energy in a magnetic field, and when you switch it off that energy has nowhere to go. The current tries to keep flowing, and the voltage at the drain spikes far above the supply, enough to destroy the MOSFET and send noise through the whole circuit. A diode across the load, pointing so that it normally blocks, gives that current a short loop to die away in.

Why the diode. Switching off a coil or motor makes its current try to continue, producing a voltage spike far above the supply. The flyback diode gives that current a harmless path. (Illustrative, not to scale.)

Fit it with the stripe (cathode) toward the positive supply. A resistive load such as an LED does not need one; anything with a coil does. Many relay and motor driver boards already include their own diode and transistor, and then you connect their input pin to a GPIO directly.

Part 5. Choosing a MOSFET

What to check on the datasheet before choosing a MOSFET. The one that catches beginners is the gate voltage: a part that needs 10 V at the gate will barely turn on from a 3.3 V pin.

The trap is the gate voltage. Many MOSFETs are designed for 10 V of gate drive and barely conduct at 3.3 V, so they run hot or do not switch. Look for a part described as logic level whose datasheet quotes a low RDS(on) at a gate voltage of 3.3 V or below, and confirm it with the datasheet rather than the part’s name.

Switching speed (PWM, for dimming or speed control) comes later in the course; for now the output is simply on or off.

Intermediate

Exercise 4: Specify a Motor Switch. A 12 V motor draws 0.8 A normally and 2.5 A when stalled. List the minimum ratings for the MOSFET (VDS, ID) and the diode, and say what else must be true of the supply and ground.

Solutions

Give each exercise an honest try before opening its solution.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Gate Surge

I = V / R: 3.3 / 100 = 33 mA (too much), 3.3 / 330 = 10 mA (borderline but acceptable), 3.3 / 1000 = 3.3 mA (gentle, but switches more slowly). 330 Ω is a common compromise.

Exercise 2: What Does Each Part Do?

(a) the gate floats while the Pico resets, so the load can switch on by itself. (b) switching the fan off produces a voltage spike that can destroy the MOSFET. (c) the gate voltage has no common reference, so the MOSFET is not reliably on or off.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: Is This MOSFET OK?

Power = I²R = 0.3² × 0.5 = 0.045 W, which is trivial. The MOSFET drops 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15 V, leaving 4.85 V for the fan, which is fine. At this current almost any logic-level MOSFET works; heat only becomes an issue at amps.

Advanced

Exercise 4: Specify a Motor Switch

MOSFET: VDS comfortably above 12 V (a 30 V part leaves margin for spikes) and ID above the 2.5 A stall current, with RDS(on) specified at 3.3 V. Diode: rated for at least the stall current (about 3 A) and a reverse voltage above 12 V, stripe toward +12 V. Supply and ground: a 12 V supply able to deliver the stall current, and its ground joined to the Pico’s ground. The Pico must not power the motor.
