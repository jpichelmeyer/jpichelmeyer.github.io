A GPIO pin has only two outputs: on and off. Yet you want to dim an LED, slow a motor, or position a servo, which all need something in between. The trick is to switch the pin on and off so fast that the load only notices the average. It is called pulse-width modulation, PWM, and the Pico has hardware that does it for you, without any of your code running.

Part 1. Fast On and Off

A PWM signal repeats a fixed period and spends a chosen fraction of it high: the duty cycle. At 25 % duty the pin is on a quarter of the time. Anything that cannot follow the rapid switching, such as the human eye, a motor’s inertia, or a filter, responds to the average, which is the duty cycle times the supply voltage.

Pulse-width modulation: switch an output fully on and fully off, very fast, and vary the fraction of time it is on (the duty cycle). A load that cannot follow the fast switching responds to the average, which is the duty cycle times the supply voltage.

Part 2. PWM in MicroPython

pwm.py MicroPython
from machine import Pin, PWM pwm = PWM(Pin(15)) pwm.freq(1000) # 1000 cycles per second pwm.duty_u16(32768) # 0..65535: this is 50 % def set_percent(pwm, pct): pwm.duty_u16(int(max(0, min(100, pct)) / 100 * 65535)) set_percent(pwm, 25) pwm.deinit() # stop PWM and release the pin

Once set, the hardware keeps producing the waveform with no help from your program; you only write when you want to change it. Constructor keywords work too: PWM(Pin(15), freq=1000, duty_u16=32768).

Part 3. Which Pins, and What They Share

How the RP2040 organises its 16 PWM outputs. Pins come in pairs that share one slice: both channels of a slice always use the same frequency, but each has its own duty cycle. Plan pin choices so loads needing different frequencies sit in different slices. (On a Pico W, GP23, GP24, GP25 and GP29 are used by the WiFi chip.)

The RP2040 has eight PWM slices, each with two channels (A and B), and any GPIO can be connected to one. The catch is that both channels of a slice share a single counter, so they share one frequency and differ only in duty. If you want a 50 Hz servo and a 20 kHz motor, put them in different slices; if you set a different frequency on a pin in the same slice, you will change the other one too.

Part 4. Choosing the Frequency

Choosing a frequency. Too slow and you see flicker or hear the motor buzz; too fast and the counter has fewer steps to divide the period into, so the duty cycle becomes coarser. The step counts below assume the RP2040’s 125 MHz clock and are approximate.

Too low and you see flicker or hear a buzz; too high and the counter has fewer steps to divide the period into, so the duty cycle becomes coarser. For LEDs, a kilohertz or two is plenty. For motors, higher than about 15 kHz is silent to most people, at the cost of resolution and slightly more switching loss.

Part 5. Dimming That Looks Linear

Eyes do not respond linearly: doubling the duty does not look twice as bright, and 10 % looks surprisingly bright. A gamma curve corrects it, so equal steps in your number look like equal steps in brightness:

pwm.py MicroPython
def gamma_duty(level, gamma=2.2): """level 0..255 -> a 16-bit duty that looks evenly spaced to the eye.""" return int((level / 255) ** gamma * 65535) for level in (0, 64, 128, 192, 255): print(level, gamma_duty(level)) # 0 0, 64 3130, 128 14386, 192 35103, 255 65535

Part 6. Servos

A hobby servo is commanded by the width of a pulse repeated every 20 ms (50 Hz): about 1 ms for one end of travel, 1.5 ms for the middle, 2 ms for the other end. Many servos accept a slightly wider range; check yours before driving it to the limits.
servo.py MicroPython
servo = PWM(Pin(14)) servo.freq(50) # a 20 ms frame def angle(deg): deg = max(0, min(180, deg)) servo.duty_ns(1_000_000 + int(deg / 180 * 1_000_000)) # 1.0 ms .. 2.0 ms pulse angle(90) # the middle

Many servos accept a wider range than 1 to 2 ms, but driving one to the end of its travel against a mechanical stop makes it strain and heat; check your servo and stay inside what it is rated for.

Powering a Servo

A hobby servo is a small motor, a gearbox and a control circuit in one case. Moving or pushing against a load, it can draw a few hundred milliamps, and more for an instant when it starts or stalls. Neither the Pico’s 3V3 pin nor a laptop’s USB port should supply that: the dips in voltage reset the Pico (a brownout), and the usual symptom is a board that reboots every time the servo moves. One unloaded servo on the bench sometimes works from USB, which is exactly why the failure is a surprise once you add load or a second servo.

Powering a servo. The servo’s power comes from its own battery pack, the signal comes from the Pico, and all three share one ground so that the signal has a reference. The battery’s negative terminal, the servo’s brown wire and a Pico GND pin are all joined.

Use the battery holder from your kit: choose the number of cells so that the voltage is within the servo’s rating (the common 9 g servo is rated for roughly 4.8 to 6 V; three alkaline cells give about 4.5 V, a little below that but usually enough, and four cells give about 6 V, the top of the range, so check the markings on your servo). Join the battery’s negative terminal to a Pico GND pin as well as to the servo, because the signal wire only means something relative to a shared ground. Moving in small steps instead of one big jump also lowers the peak current.

When a servo is holding position but you no longer need force, you can stop its signal with servo.duty_ns(0): it goes limp, stops drawing holding current and stops buzzing, and you start the pulses again when you want it to move.

One last point connects to the next lesson: a PWM pin still only supplies a few milliamps. To dim anything bigger than an LED, or to run a fan, a pump or a motor, the pin must control a switch that carries the real current. The PWM frequency then matters too, because the switch has to turn on and off cleanly at that rate.

Solutions

Give each exercise an honest try before opening its solution.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: Average Voltage

Average = duty × 3.3 V: 0.2 × 3.3 = 0.66 V, 0.6 × 3.3 = 1.98 V, 0.9 × 3.3 = 2.97 V.

Exercise 2: Slice Conflict

GP14 and GP15 are the A and B channels of the same slice (slice 7), so they share one frequency: setting one changes the other. Move one signal to a pin in a different slice, for example the motor input to GP12 (slice 6) or GP16 (slice 0).

Intermediate

Exercise 3: A Smooth Fade
fade.py MicroPython
from time import sleep_ms def fade(pwm, start, end, ms): steps = 50 for i in range(steps + 1): pct = start + (end - start) * i / steps set_percent(pwm, pct) sleep_ms(ms // steps) fade(pwm, 0, 100, 1000) # up over one second fade(pwm, 100, 0, 1000) # and back down

Fifty steps is smooth to the eye. Note that sleep_ms blocks; in an agent with other work to do you would advance one step per call from the main loop instead.

Advanced

Exercise 4: Servo Mapping
servo.py MicroPython
def angle_to_ns(deg, lo_ns=1_000_000, hi_ns=2_000_000): deg = max(0, min(180, deg)) return lo_ns + int((hi_ns - lo_ns) * deg / 180) for d in (0, 45, 90, 200): print(d, angle_to_ns(d)) # 0 1000000, 45 1250000, 90 1500000, 200 2000000

The 200° request is clamped to 180°, giving 2.0 ms. Putting the limits in parameters lets you narrow them to your own servo’s safe range without changing the function.
