Quantization was about how precisely each reading captures a voltage. This lesson is about a separate question: how often you take a reading at all, and why getting that wrong can make a signal look like something it isn't, not just a blurrier version of the truth, but a confidently wrong one.

Part 1. Sampling Rate

Calling sensor.read_u16() once gives you a single number, a snapshot at that instant. Call it repeatedly, on a timer, and you get a sampled version of a continuously changing signal, a sequence of snapshots instead of the real continuous curve. How often you sample is the sampling rate, usually measured in Hz (samples per second).

shell MicroPython
from machine import ADC, Pin from time import sleep sensor = ADC(Pin(26)) while True: print(sensor.read_u16()) sleep(0.1) # sampling at 10 Hz -- ten readings per second
Sampling is reading on a clock. The time between dots is the period T; the rate is 1/T. Everything the signal does between two dots is invisible to the program.

Part 2. What Goes Wrong When You Sample Too Slowly

Picture a signal that's genuinely oscillating, swinging up and down repeatedly, faster than you're sampling it. Each sample only sees where the wave happens to be at that instant, and if your samples consistently land at similar points in each cycle, the sequence of readings can trace out a slow, smooth wave that was never actually there, a completely fabricated signal called an alias.

Same fast wave in both rows. Enough samples per cycle (top) and you can see it's oscillating. Too few samples per cycle (bottom) and the dots connect into a completely different, much slower apparent wave -- an alias. Nothing is broken electrically; the samples are each individually accurate, and still add up to a lie.

Part 3. The Nyquist-Shannon Theorem

The fix has a name and a precise statement: to faithfully capture a signal, you must sample at more than twice the fastest frequency present in that signal. That threshold, twice the signal's highest frequency, is called the Nyquist rate; sample any slower and some information is gone for good, no amount of clever processing afterward recovers it.

This isn't specific to electronics, it's a statement about sampling anything that varies over time. It's exactly why, for example, audio CDs sample at 44.1 kHz: human hearing tops out around 20 kHz, and 44.1 kHz comfortably clears twice that.

Part 4. Why This Matters for a Slow Classroom Sensor

Most sensors in your kit, temperature, light level, change gradually, on the order of seconds, not thousands of times per second. For those, Nyquist is almost never the binding constraint, sampling once or twice a second is overkill-safe. It becomes the first thing to check, though, the moment a sensor changes quickly: a sound sensor, a vibration sensor, or anything meant to catch a fast mechanical event. Before picking a sampling interval for any new sensor this semester, ask what the fastest meaningful change in that signal actually is, then make sure your sampling comfortably clears twice that.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: How Fast Did It Really Go?
main.py MicroPython
from machine import ADC, Pin from time import ticks_ms, ticks_diff, sleep_ms adc = ADC(Pin(26)) n = 0 start = ticks_ms() while ticks_diff(ticks_ms(), start) < 2000: adc.read_u16() n += 1 sleep_ms(10) print(n, "samples in 2 s")

You will see a little under 200: the read and the loop overhead add to every 10 ms sleep, so the real rate is slightly below 100 Hz. Measure rather than assume.

Advanced

Exercise 4: Fooled by Mains Hum

Apparent frequency = |f − k·fs| for the nearest whole k. 60 Hz sampled at 50 Hz looks like |60 − 50| = 10 Hz. 8 Hz sampled at 10 Hz looks like |8 − 10| = 2 Hz. In both cases a fast, harmless disturbance shows up as a slow, convincing-looking drift.
