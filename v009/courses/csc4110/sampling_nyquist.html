The sampling rate you choose decides what you can and cannot know about a signal. This lesson covers sampling at a steady rate, why the Nyquist limit exists, what aliasing looks like when you break it, and how to check that your loop and your data budget can keep up.

Part 1. Sampling

An ADC reading is one snapshot. To follow a signal you take snapshots repeatedly, at a steady rate. The rate is the sampling frequency f_s in samples per second (hertz), and the time between snapshots is the sample period T_s = 1 / f_s. A light sensor read 100 times a second has T_s = 10 ms.

How fast is fast enough? It depends on how quickly the thing you measure changes. A room’s temperature changes over minutes, so once a second is plenty. The vibration of a motor changes dozens of times a second, and reading it once a second would tell you nothing.

The same 2 Hz signal sampled at three rates. At 20 samples per second the dots follow the curve closely. At 8 per second the shape is still clear. At 3 per second the dots no longer show a 2 Hz wave at all: they trace out something much slower, which is the danger described next.

Part 2. The Nyquist-Shannon Rule

There is a hard limit. To capture a signal whose highest frequency is f_max, you must sample at more than twice f_max: f_s > 2 × f_max. Half the sampling rate, f_s / 2, is called the Nyquist frequency: the highest frequency that can be told apart at that rate.

Frequencies above the Nyquist frequency do not simply vanish. They fold back and appear as a lower frequency that was never there, an alias. The sampled data looks perfectly normal, so the error is invisible unless you know to look for it.

Aliasing. A 9 Hz wave sampled 10 times a second gives dots that sit exactly on a slow 1 Hz wave (orange, inverted). Nothing in the data says this is wrong: you would swear the signal was 1 Hz. The Nyquist frequency here is only 5 Hz, and 9 Hz is above it.
alias.py MicroPython
def alias_hz(f, fs): """The frequency a pure tone of f Hz appears to have when sampled at fs Hz.""" k = round(f / fs) # the nearest multiple of fs return abs(f - k * fs) for f in (3, 6, 9, 12): print(f, "Hz at fs = 10 ->", alias_hz(f, 10), "Hz") # 3 -> 3 6 -> 4 9 -> 1 12 -> 2
What frequency you see for each true frequency when sampling at 10 Hz. Up to 5 Hz (the Nyquist frequency) the signal is reported correctly. Above it the reported value folds back down: 6 Hz appears as 4 Hz, 9 Hz as 1 Hz, and 12 Hz as 2 Hz (the orange dots).

Part 3. Defending Against Aliasing

  • Sample fast enough. In practice use at least 5 to 10 times the fastest change you care about, not the bare 2 times of the theory, because real signals are not clean sine waves.
  • Remove what you do not want before sampling. An analog low-pass filter (a resistor and capacitor) ahead of the pin stops fast noise from folding down.
  • Oversample, then average. Sample several times faster than you need, then average groups of samples into one output. The averaging acts as a digital filter, and it also reduces noise (next lesson).

Part 4. Sampling at a Steady Rate

Samples must be evenly spaced for any of this to hold. A loop that does read(); sleep_ms(10) is slightly slower than 100 Hz, because the reading and processing take time on top of the sleep. A better pattern schedules each sample against a fixed deadline, so small delays do not pile up:

sampler.py MicroPython
from machine import ADC from time import ticks_us, ticks_add, ticks_diff, sleep_us adc = ADC(26) def run_sampler(read, hz, n): """Take n samples at hz. Return (timestamps_us, values, overruns).""" period = 1_000_000 // hz deadline = ticks_us() times, values, overruns = [], [], 0 for _ in range(n): times.append(ticks_us()) values.append(read()) deadline = ticks_add(deadline, period) # the NEXT sample is due one period later wait = ticks_diff(deadline, ticks_us()) if wait > 0: sleep_us(wait) else: overruns += 1 # we were too slow: it was already due return times, values, overruns times, values, over = run_sampler(adc.read_u16, 100, 200) print("overruns:", over)

Each deadline is computed from the previous deadline, not from when the loop happened to finish, so a late sample does not push all later ones back. If the work takes longer than the period the sampler cannot keep up, and the overruns count tells you so. That is a number worth printing.

Part 5. The Budget

Sampling rate also sets two budgets that must balance. The time budget is the sample period: everything you do per sample (read the sensors, filter, format, maybe send) must fit inside it. The data budget is the rate of bytes you create: f_s × channels × bytes per sample, which has to fit through whatever you send it to.

A time budget (illustrative proportions, not measurements). In the top row the work finishes well inside the period. In the bottom row the same period is overrun because the work takes too long, so samples arrive late and unevenly. Measure your real numbers on your own board.
profile.py MicroPython
from time import ticks_us, ticks_diff def profile(fn, n=200): """Average and worst-case run time of fn in microseconds.""" total = worst = 0 for _ in range(n): t0 = ticks_us() fn() dt = ticks_diff(ticks_us(), t0) total += dt worst = max(worst, dt) return total // n, worst

The worst case matters more than the average: a loop that usually takes 2 ms but occasionally takes 12 ms will overrun a 10 ms period. When you design a node, add up the worst cases of everything inside the loop and compare with the period.

Exercise 2: Choose a Rate. A motor vibrates at up to 40 Hz and you want to see its shape, not just detect it. What is the theoretical minimum sampling rate, and what would you actually choose?

Intermediate

Exercise 3: The Budget. You sample 3 analog channels, 2 bytes each, at 250 Hz. How many bytes per second does that produce, and how long is the period? If reading, filtering and formatting take 1.8 ms in the worst case, how much slack is left?

Advanced

Exercise 4: Time a Loop Body. Use profile to time a function that does a 4-point average of an ADC. Test the profile function itself without hardware by giving it a fake function that advances a simulated clock.

Warm Up

Exercise 1: What Do You See?

alias_hz(7, 10) = |7 − 10| = 3 Hz. It is not safe: 7 Hz is above the Nyquist frequency (5 Hz), so the data shows a 3 Hz vibration that does not exist.

Exercise 2: Choose a Rate

The minimum is anything above 2 × 40 = 80 Hz. For a usable waveform choose 5 to 10 times the highest frequency: 200 to 400 Hz.

Intermediate

Exercise 3: The Budget

Data: 3 × 2 × 250 = 1500 B/s. Period: 1 / 250 = 4 ms. Slack: 4 − 1.8 = 2.2 ms, which is 55 % of the period, a healthy margin that leaves room for sending.

Advanced

Exercise 4: Time a Loop Body

On the board, profile(lambda: sum(adc.read_u16() for _ in range(4)) // 4) returns the average and worst time for one filtered reading. To test the timing code itself, substitute a controllable clock:

test_profile.py MicroPython
clock = [0] ticks_us = lambda: clock[0] # replace the clock the function looks up def ticks_diff(a, b): return a - b durations = iter([2000, 2100, 1900, 6000] * 50) def fake_work(): clock[0] += next(durations) # each call "takes" the next duration print(profile(fake_work, 200)) # (3000, 6000): average 3 ms, worst 6 ms

The average (3000 µs) looks comfortable against a 4 ms period, but the worst case (6000 µs) is an overrun. Reporting both numbers is what exposes it.
