Lesson

Most series can't be summed in closed form the way a geometric or telescoping series can — the best you can usually do is decide whether $\sum a_i$ converges, by comparing it to a series you already understand. One family worth knowing by heart first:

$p$-series

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{i^p}$ converges if $p>1$, and diverges if $p\le 1$. The case $p=1$ is the harmonic series, $\sum \frac1i$ — it diverges, despite $\frac1i\to 0$.

Direct Comparison Test

Suppose $0 \le a_i \le b_i$ for all $i$ beyond some point.

• If $\sum b_i$ converges, so does $\sum a_i$ (it's squeezed under a convergent series).

• If $\sum a_i$ diverges, so does $\sum b_i$ (it's forced above a divergent one).

Limit Comparison Test

Suppose $a_i, b_i > 0$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty} \frac{a_i}{b_i} = c$ for some finite $c>0$. Then $\sum a_i$ and $\sum b_i$ either both converge or both diverge. This sidesteps having to find an inequality that goes the right direction, which the Direct Comparison Test needs.

Example 1 — Direct Comparison

Does $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{i^2+i}$ converge?

Since $i^2+i > i^2$, we get $\dfrac{1}{i^2+i} < \dfrac{1}{i^2}$. The $p$-series $\sum \frac1{i^2}$ converges ($p=2>1$), so by Direct Comparison, $\sum \frac1{i^2+i}$ converges too.

Example 2 — Limit Comparison

Does $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{3i^2+1}{i^4+2i+5}$ converge?

For large $i$, the dominant behavior is $\frac{3i^2}{i^4}=\frac{3}{i^2}$, so compare against $b_i=\frac1{i^2}$: $\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{a_i}{b_i} = \lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{(3i^2+1)i^2}{i^4+2i+5} = 3$, a finite positive number. Since $\sum \frac1{i^2}$ converges, the Limit Comparison Test says $\sum a_i$ converges too.
