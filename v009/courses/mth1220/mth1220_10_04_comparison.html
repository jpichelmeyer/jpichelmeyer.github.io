Lesson

Most series can't be summed in closed form the way a geometric or telescoping series can — the best you can usually do is decide whether $\sum a_n$ converges, by comparing it to a series you already understand. One family worth knowing by heart first:

$p$-series

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p}$ converges if $p>1$, and diverges if $p\le 1$. The case $p=1$ is the harmonic series, $\sum \frac1n$ — it diverges, despite $\frac1n\to 0$.

Direct Comparison Test

Suppose $0 \le a_n \le b_n$ for all $n$ beyond some point.

• If $\sum b_n$ converges, so does $\sum a_n$ (it's squeezed under a convergent series).

• If $\sum a_n$ diverges, so does $\sum b_n$ (it's forced above a divergent one).

Limit Comparison Test

Suppose $a_n, b_n > 0$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = c$ for some finite $c>0$. Then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ either both converge or both diverge. This sidesteps having to find an inequality that goes the right direction, which the Direct Comparison Test needs.

Example 1 — Direct Comparison

Does $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2+n}$ converge?

Since $n^2+n > n^2$, we get $\dfrac{1}{n^2+n} < \dfrac{1}{n^2}$. The $p$-series $\sum \frac1{n^2}$ converges ($p=2>1$), so by Direct Comparison, $\sum \frac1{n^2+n}$ converges too.

Example 2 — Limit Comparison

Does $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{3n^2+1}{n^4+2n+5}$ converge?

For large $n$, the dominant behavior is $\frac{3n^2}{n^4}=\frac{3}{n^2}$, so compare against $b_n=\frac1{n^2}$: $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(3n^2+1)n^2}{n^4+2n+5} = 3$, a finite positive number. Since $\sum \frac1{n^2}$ converges, the Limit Comparison Test says $\sum a_n$ converges too.
