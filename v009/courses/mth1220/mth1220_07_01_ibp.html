Lesson

Substitution reverses the Chain Rule. Integration by parts reverses the Product Rule, and it's the tool for integrands that are a product of two unrelated-looking functions, like $x\,e^x$ or $x\ln x$, where no substitution simplifies both factors at once.

The formula

$$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$$

Starting from $\int f(x)g'(x)\,dx$, choose $u=f(x)$ and $dv=g'(x)\,dx$, then compute $du$ (differentiate $u$) and $v$ (antidifferentiate $dv$). The goal is for $\int v\,du$ to be easier than the integral you started with.

The rough heuristic LIATE (Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential) ranks what usually makes a good choice for $u$, earlier categories first — a log or inverse-trig factor should almost always become $u$, since neither has an elementary antiderivative you'd want to be stuck computing as $v$.

Example 1

Find $\displaystyle\int x\,e^x\,dx$.

Let $u=x$ (algebraic, ranks above exponential in LIATE), $dv=e^x\,dx$. Then $du=dx$, $v=e^x$.

$$\int x\,e^x\,dx = x\,e^x - \int e^x\,dx = x\,e^x - e^x + C$$

Example 2 — when $dv$ has to be the whole integrand

Find $\displaystyle\int \ln x\,dx$.

There's only one factor, so let $u=\ln x$, $dv=dx$. Then $du=\frac1x dx$, $v=x$.

$$\int \ln x\,dx = x\ln x - \int x\cdot\frac1x\,dx = x\ln x - \int 1\,dx = x\ln x - x + C$$

Example 3 — applying it twice

Find $\displaystyle\int x^2 e^x\,dx$.

Let $u=x^2$, $dv=e^x dx$; then $du=2x\,dx$, $v=e^x$: $\int x^2e^x dx = x^2e^x - 2\int x\,e^x\,dx$. The remaining integral is Example 1, already solved: $x^2e^x - 2(xe^x-e^x)+C = e^x(x^2-2x+2)+C$.
