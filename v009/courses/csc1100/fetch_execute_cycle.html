The loops lesson warned you that a broken while loop can hang the whole page, and told you to just reload if it happens. That was an honest warning, but not a real explanation. Here's the actual one.

Part 1. One Instruction at a Time

A CPU core runs a constant cycle: fetch the next instruction, figure out what it means, carry it out, then move to the next one. Fetch, decode, execute, repeat, billions of times a second, but always one instruction actually being carried out at any given instant on that core. Nothing about that cycle has any built-in notion of "come back to this later," it just keeps moving forward, instruction after instruction, for as long as the program tells it to.

Part 2. Why an Infinite Loop Actually Freezes the Page

The browser tab this page is running in is, at its core, executing on a single thread, the same one-thing-at-a-time model as a CPU core, just at a higher level: rendering the page, responding to your clicks, and running the Python you type all compete for the same single sequence of instructions being carried out. When your code hits a while loop whose condition never goes false, that thread is permanently stuck fetching and executing the loop's instructions, over and over, forever. It's not choosing to ignore the Stop button, there's no way for it to even notice the click happened, responding to a click is itself just another instruction waiting in line, and the line never moves again once the loop takes over.

This is the same failure from a different angle than the recursion lesson's stack overflow: that one runs out of a finite resource (memory); this one just never reaches the point where it would hand control back to anything else at all.

Part 3. Why a for Loop Over range() Doesn't Have This Problem

A while loop's condition gets checked fresh, from scratch, every single cycle, with nothing inherently limiting how many times that can happen. A for loop over range(n) is different in a way that actually matters here: the total number of iterations is fixed the moment the loop starts, decided by n, not re-derived from some condition that could keep evaluating to true indefinitely.

fetch_execute.py Python
total = 0 for i in range(1000000): total += 1 print(total)

A million iterations is a lot of fetch-execute cycles, but it's a known, finite number of them, decided in advance. It'll take a moment, but it's structurally guaranteed to finish. A while loop with a broken condition offers no such guarantee at all.
