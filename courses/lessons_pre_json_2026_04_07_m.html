Definition?
Definition (Intuitive): An algorithm is a set of instructions that is designed to accomplish a task.

This is not very formal (and so hard to do much with), so people have tried to take more formal and mathematical approaches. Among them, there have been three notable perspectives:

  • Lambda Calculus (Alonzo Church). An algorithm is a series of applications of mathematical functions and transformations.
  • Recursive Functions (Kurt Godel, Stephen Kleene). An algorithm is a set of simple mathematical functions that can be built into increasingly complex functions through repetition.
  • Turing Machines (Alan Turing). An algorithm is a set of rules for a hypothetical machine that reads and writes symbols on an infinite tape.

All of these approaches are independent and equivalent ways of defining algorithms and computability. We will look at how a Turing Machine works later.

Pseudocode

Suppose you are nuclear engineer who is tasked with maintaining a certain part of a nuclear reactor. You notice that the part you are supposed to watch is acting unusually. You know you need to add a particular type of coolant, but can't remember which one or how much and if you are off by even a little bit, the part could fail and the entire facility could explode. You ask your supervisor and their response is: 'Just add a little of the blue stuff.'

How helpful was your supervisor's direction?

Pseudocode is a way of trying to make an algorithm explicit enough that there is no ambiguity as to what should be done, but not so explicit that it becomes difficult to parse, requiring more mental energy and time than the process is worth.

No universal convention. Large range in degree of formality. One example below. 

Input: a collection A of numbers a1, a2, a3, ..., an\n
Outpt: a number ai from the collection such that aj is less than or equal to ai for all j=1,2,...,n\n
\n
FIND-MAXIMUM(A)\n
1  max <-- A[1]\n
2  FOR i from 2 to A.length\n
3      IF A[i] > max THEN\n
4          max <-- A[i]\n
5      END IF\n
6  END FOR\n
7  RETURN  max
Pseudocode: Examples

Here is the same task (addition of two numbers), but approached in three very different ways.


Version: Accumulation 
Input: a,b\n
Output: sum of a and b\n
\n
ADD(a,b)\n
1  sum <-- 0\n
2  sum <-- sum + a>\n
3  sum <-- sum + b>\n
4  RETURN sum

Version: Iteration 
Input: a,b\n
Output: sum of a and b\n
\n
ADD(a,b)\n
1  sum <-- a\n
2  REPEAT b times\n
3      sum <-- sum + 1\n
4  END REPEAT\n
5  RETURN sum
Version: Recursion 
Input: a,b\n
Output: sum of a and b\n
\n
ADD(a,b)\n
1  IF b == 0 THEN\n
2      RETURN a\n
3  END IF\n
4  RETURN Add(a+1, b-1)

Tedium

Try carrying out the steps of the algorithm below very carefully and methodically. 

Input: sequence A = {a1, a2, ..., aN} of integers
Output: a reordering B = {b1, b2, ..., bN} of a1, a2, ..., aN such that bi <= bj whenever i<= j 
\n
InsertionSort(A)\n
1  FOR j from 2 to n\n
2      key <-- A[j]\n
3      i <-- j-1\n
4      WHILE i > 0 and A[i] > key\n
5          A[i+1] <-- A[i]\n
6          i <--i-1\n
7      END WHILE\n
8      A[i+1] <-- key\n
9  END FOR\n
10 RETURN A

As we start to formalize algorithms, carrying them out becomes quite tedious. If only we had a happy little helper who would gladly punch away at these highly precise, yet highly monotonous microtasks. If only...

But first we should maybe ask outselves whether having such a helper would even be useful. In particular, could we even reasonably guarantee that it would be competent? That is, that it would be able to solve any algorithm that we threw at it?

To that end, we now turn to the Turing machine.

Why do we care about Turing machines?
default.png<

The three main approaches of formalism (Church's Lambda Calculus, Godel's Recursive Functions, and Turing's Turing machines') are all equivalent. Each approach has at least one instance where its perspective is uniquely helpful. Thinking about algorithmic formalism through the Turing machine perspective is especially helpful when looking at hardware (which we will do in the next unit).

Church-Turing thesis: Our intuitive notion of an algorithm is perfectly captured by the formal mathematical concept of a Turing machine.

The Church-Turing thesis is unproven (and unprovable), but most computer scientists believe it. From this came the notion of Turing Completeness for programming languages, whereby if a language can simulate a Turing machine, it can perform any computation that any other computer can (and by the Church-Turing thesis, can carry out any algorithm that a human could).
